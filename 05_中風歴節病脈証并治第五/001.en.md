# Clause 1 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Wind disease should be hemiplegia; if only the arm cannot be used, it is impediment. A feeble, rapid pulse is caused by wind stroke.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

This discusses severity. Rather than detailing the signs of wind disease, it focuses on half the body versus the arm, distinguishing serious from not serious, so as to avoid mistreatment. Wind disease originally arises from yang deficiency with wind attacking it. Yang deficiency produces impediment, which concerns one limb or one joint. As [the *Neijing*] says, "each enters by its gate" (lacuna) even if the wind does not reach the whole body, there should still be hemiplegia. "Cannot be used" (不遂) means not functioning. If only the arm cannot be used, it is like one branch of a tree; how does it concern the yang qi of the whole body? Hence "this is malaria [error for impediment]." Impediment means closure, numbness: the qi of one joint happens to be closed and numb. Then, verified by the pulse, it must be feeble and rapid. Feeble is feebleness of yang; rapid is the rapidity of wind. "Caused by wind stroke" means that wind takes advantage of deficiency, enters, and then causes hemiplegia.

Discussion: For cold damage in winter, Zhongjing divided treatment into "cold damaging the ying-nutrient," "wind damaging the wei-defense," and "wind and cold both damaging," yet titled the chapters throughout as cold damage. This is because when essence is not stored in winter, cold invades the flesh and bones and kills most swiftly. There are cases of wind damage among them, but they are subsumed under cold, meaning that wind is not enough to kill and it is really winter cold that kills easily. In discussing wind stroke, he assigns it entirely to wind yet still speaks without excluding cold. When pathogen gradually attaches to a person's skin, though generally due to wind, the wind carries cold; so it is subsumed under wind. This means that the cold of the other three seasons does not kill at once, but gradually deepening wind kills before one notices.

So in the first section Zhongjing sets out the words "wind stroke," taking a feeble, rapid pulse as the main sign of right deficiency and pathogen exuberance. The second section discusses the floating, tight [pulse] that is cold, followed by Houshi Hei san (Marquis Hou's Black Powder) for cases where the pathogen has not yet invaded the heart, showing the method of filling the empty orifices, similar in principle to [Xiao] Jianzhong [tang]. The third section discusses the slow, slowdown [pulse] that is wind, followed by Fengyin tang (Fuinto; Wind-Drawing Decoction) as a formula to remove heat, showing that wind, which moves well and changes often, causes paralysis and epilepsy always through heat, similar in idea to Baihu [tang]. Next comes Fangji Dihuang tang (Boijioto; Stephania and Rehmannia Decoction) for cases where heat has already invaded the heart, showing the method of clearing the heart and calming the spirit, similar in principle to "one must first rescue the interior." But wind stroke disease, whether cold or wind predominates, is generally due to deficiency. So from start to finish the word "deficiency" is never absent, though the depth naturally differs.

## Notes

The general clause on wind stroke (*zhongfeng*, corresponding to stroke). It distinguishes hemiplegia, which is wind stroke, from inability to use only the arm, which is impediment.

- A feeble (healthy qi deficiency) and rapid (pathogen exuberance) pulse is said to be the pulse of wind stroke.
- "Wind stroke" here differs from Taiyang wind stroke in the *Shanghan lun* (an exterior pattern from pathogenic wind); it corresponds to what was later called stroke.
- Xu Bin's 此為瘧 ("this is malaria") appears to be an error for the text's 此為痺 ("this is impediment"). His "Discussion" surveys the structure of this chapter (the wind stroke pulse and three formulas).
