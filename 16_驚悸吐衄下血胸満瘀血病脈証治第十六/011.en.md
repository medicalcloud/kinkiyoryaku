# Clause 11 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

When a sick person seems to have heat, with vexation and fullness, a dry mouth with thirst, but the pulse, on the contrary, shows no heat, this is yin hidden [base: 状 "appearance"]; it is static blood, and it should be purged.

Pathogenic Fire: Guizhi qu Shaoyao jia Shuqi Muli Longgu Jiuni tang (Cinnamon Twig Decoction minus Peony plus Dichroa Leaf, Oyster Shell, and Dragon Bone Counterflow-Rescuing Decoction) governs it.

Guizhi Jiuni tang formula

Guizhi 2 liang (remove the bark); gancao 3 liang (mix-fried); shengjiang 6 liang; muli 5 liang (dry-fried); longgu 4 liang; dazao 12 pieces; shuqi 3 liang (washed to remove the rank odor).

Powder the above. With 1 dou 2 sheng of water first boil the shuqi, reducing by 2 sheng; add the other medicinals, then [other sources: boil] down to 3 sheng, remove the dregs, and take 1 sheng warm.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

These two clauses say that a person apparently healthy, without disease of exterior or interior, may have static blood, with signs and pulse not corresponding, as here. The chest is the upper jiao, receiving qi from the middle jiao; lips, mouth, and tongue are all governed by spleen and stomach; so the *Qianjin* says, "The mouth is *wu*, the lips and tongue are *ji*; circling the central palace, they flourish at the tongue." Now there is stasis in the central palace, so the central qi is not clear; hot qi fumes the upper jiao, producing chest fullness, and follows the orifices of the flesh, producing withered lips, a green-blue tongue, and a dry mouth. Wanting to rinse the mouth with water is dryness of blood and qi; not wanting to swallow means there has never been heat in the chest. No cold or heat means neither exterior nor interior. Moreover, a feeble pulse is close to great deficiency, and slow arrival also means deficiency without heat. When the sanjiao is distended, the qi should fill the skin; now the skin outside the abdomen is not full, yet one feels uncomfortably distended and says "I feel full"; there is stagnation. What is this if not static blood? Hence "there is static blood."

If the patient seems to have heat, it is an appearance of oppression — the "vexation and fullness, dry mouth with thirst" below. If there were truly interior heat, the pulse should be rapid, but on the contrary there is no heat — no surging, rapid pulse is seen. Is it not that something yin is hidden within, making yin fire affect the upper part? Hence "this is yin hidden." What is the yin? Static blood. Stasis belongs to the tangible and must be purged; hence "it should be purged." These three characters seem to sum up both sections, but the earlier section speaks of chest fullness, not wanting to swallow water, and slow arrival of the pulse; it is not only static blood, and if there is much cold inside, cold purgative medicinals cannot be used, and the method of removing stasis should be further considered; so it does not say generally "may be purged."

This formula treats fright — fright and mania with restlessness within a disease, not the settling of the heart as with Anshen wan or Zhenjing wan. The chapter on running piglet qi says that the four kinds of disease including fright and fear are all contracted through fright and fear; but disease from being startled by a hollow noise can be cured by settling what floats. If, from moxibustion fire, there is heat and fright, so that pathogen binds in the chest with fright, mania, and restlessness, one must center on driving out and dispersing the pathogen in the chest. So it is labeled "pathogenic fire," showing that the chest, the residence of clear yang, has had its yang lost through forcing with fire, and the spirit light is scattered and disordered. So guizhi, gancao, shengjiang, and dazao diffuse the original yang of the upper jiao, and the small fire is extinguished by itself. Fright must involve stasis and binding, so shuqi, the sprout of changshan, is added to break blood and treat bound pathogen in the chest; and longgu, sweet, astringent, and neutral, with muli, sour, salty, and cold, one yang and one yin, make heart and kidney interact and calm the scattered spirit. Shaoyao is removed from Guizhi tang because the disease is not in liver or spleen, and its sour astringency entering the abdomen is unwanted.

Discussion: Fright and palpitations seem to belong to diseases of the spirit light, but Zhongjing places them at the head of vomiting of blood, nosebleed, bleeding below, and static blood; so we know this formula emphasizes treating stasis and binding to restore the yang, not settling and weighing down. So treating fright centers entirely on diffusing yang, dispersing binding, calming the heart, and removing counterflow. As for palpitations, the blame is placed solely on phlegm, with banxia and mahuang releasing the yang and transforming phlegm as the mainstay. That is, if the bound pathogen is not removed, fright has no way to be calmed, and if the right yang is not released, the pathogen of palpitations does not leave.

[Part of Xu Bin's formula:] guizhi 3 liang (remove the bark); gancao 2 liang (mix-fried); shengjiang 3 liang; muli 5 liang (dry-fried).

## Notes

A paragraph combining a clause stating that apparent heat with vexation, fullness, and thirst but no heat in the pulse is due to static blood and should be purged, and a clause using Guizhi qu Shaoyao jia Shuqi Muli Longgu Jiuni tang for fright and mania due to pathogenic fire (mistreatment with fire).

- Jiuni tang is the same formula as in the middle Taiyang chapter of the *Shanghan lun* (corresponding to clause 112) for yang collapse with fright and mania after sweat has been forced with fire.
- For 状 in 此為陰状, most other sources read 伏 ("yin hidden"), and Xu Bin also reads 陰伏.
- Most other sources give guizhi 3 liang, gancao 2 liang, and shengjiang 3 liang, differing from the base.
