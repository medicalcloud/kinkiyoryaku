# Clause 2 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

The instep yang pulse is tight [base: hard] and rapid. Rapid means heat; with heat, grain is dispersed. Tight means cold; on eating there is fullness. A floating cubit pulse means damage to the kidney; a tight instep yang pulse means damage to the spleen. When wind and cold contend, eating grain causes dizziness; the grain qi is not dispersed; the stomach suffers from turbidity; the turbid qi flows downward; urination is blocked; the yin receives the cold, heat flows into the bladder, and the whole body turns yellow. This is called grain jaundice.

A black forehead, slight sweating, heat in the centers of the hands and feet, breaking out at dusk, tension of the bladder, and spontaneously free urination: this is called sexual-taxation jaundice. If the abdomen is like water [disease], it is incurable.

Anguish and heat in the heart, inability to eat, and frequent desire to vomit: this is called alcohol jaundice.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

This section speaks of the interdependence of pulse and signs in grain jaundice. The flesh is governed by spleen and stomach, and yellowing arises from damage to them. The instep yang is the pulse governing spleen and stomach, so the blame is placed there. If tight and rapid: rapid is heat, and heat disperses grain; carrying tightness means the root is cold and the branch hot. The root first received cold, and cold causes fullness — meaning that although grain is easily dispersed, there is fullness at times. This is a stomach disease, but the kidney is the gate of the stomach; if the stomach cannot disperse grain, the kidney must have been damaged, so the dragon fire cannot rise to rot and ripen the five grains. So tracing the source, it reaches the floating [cubit] pulse: floating at the cubit damages the kidney, and tightness at the instep yang damages the spleen. When spleen and kidney are both damaged, wind and cold contend; the spleen cannot convey essence to liver and lung, and the disease qi follows the channels upward to the eyes, so eating causes dizziness (the *Qianjin* adds "spinning"). Since the spleen cannot convey essence upward, the grain qi naturally is not dispersed. Then the clear yang qi in the stomach does not rise, and one suffers from turbidity. Urination comes out through qi transformation; when ascent and descent fail and turbid qi flows down, urine has no qi to transform it, and depressed heat interferes, so it gradually becomes blocked. Why is this? The yin of the viscera receives damage from cold, and guest heat flows into the bladder. The bladder is taiyang, commanding the yang of the flesh and exterior of the whole body; when cold and heat depress each other, the whole body turns yellow. The disease originally is damage by wind and cold involving spleen and kidney; if the grain qi were dispersed, the right would suffice to overcome the evil. Now it is not dispersed, the stomach becomes turbid, and turbidity leads to yellowing; so grain is not the cause of yellowing but is in fact its main medium. Hence "grain jaundice," to distinguish it from jaundice that does not interfere with grain.

This says that although yellowing always arises from spleen damage, the source of the damage may be the kidney. Its sign is always a black forehead. The forehead is the region of the heart; when kidney evil is heavy, water overcomes fire, and black, the color of water, appears in the region of fire. Laogong in the hand belongs to the heart and Yongquan in the foot to the kidney; when the kidney is vacuous and water and fire do not aid each other, there is heat; "center" refers generally to the hands and feet. Human respiration moves through the yang twenty-five times by day and through the yin twenty-five times by night, fifty circuits a day around the body; at dusk it meets *you*, which governs the kidney. Because there is original vacuity heat, the defensive qi combines with it and it breaks out as heat in the hands and feet. The bladder is the bowel of the kidney; when the yin of the kidney viscus is vacuous, the outer bowel is naturally tense. But though tense, its water issues from the high source, not comparable to heat flowing into the bladder, so urination is unobstructed and spontaneously free. Under the later sexual-taxation formula there are further signs — fever in the late afternoon with aversion to cold instead, fullness of the lesser abdomen, yellowing of the whole body — not listed here; one should know that fever, aversion to cold, abdominal fullness, and yellowing may occur in other patterns, whereas these are unique to kidney disease. So when these several signs are seen, it is named sexual-taxation jaundice, meaning excessive sexual activity causing sexual taxation. But if the abdomen is like water, the spleen essence is not held, and both prenatal and postnatal [foundations] are cut off; so it is incurable.

This says that although yellow is the spleen's color, there are cases due to alcohol. Alcohol is mostly damp and yang in nature, so the damage is in the upper burner. When the heart is afflicted by damp-heat, it is hot, anguished, and restless. When hot qi sickens the stomach and the evil does not digest grain, one cannot eat; when food is not transformed and the qi counterflows upward, there is frequent desire to vomit. The later note on grain jaundice also has the phrase "unrest in the heart and chest." But since these signs do not arise after eating grain, we know it is alcohol jaundice.

## Notes

A clause dividing jaundice into three kinds: grain jaundice, sexual-taxation jaundice, and alcohol jaundice.

- Grain jaundice: spleen and stomach damaged by wind and cold, grain not dispersed, dizziness on eating, turbidity in the stomach, blocked urination, yellowing of the whole body.
- Sexual-taxation jaundice: jaundice of kidney vacuity from excessive sexual activity; black forehead, hot palms and soles, breaking out at dusk, free urination. If water collects in the abdomen, it is incurable.
- Alcohol jaundice: jaundice from alcohol, with anguish and heat in the chest, inability to eat, and nausea.
- For 堅 in 趺陽脈堅而数, all other sources read 緊 ("tight"), which also fits the following text; the translation notes it.
- The base has errors in 眩, 濁濁, and 小便; these are emended because all other sources agree (see the emendation notes).
