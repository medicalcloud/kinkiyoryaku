# Clause 3 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Question: In a disease of cough with counterflow, how does one know by the pulse that it is lung abscess? There should be pus and blood, and spitting it out means death; what is its pulse like?

The master said: The pulse at the inch opening is feeble and rapid. Feeble means wind; rapid means heat. Feeble means sweating; rapid means aversion to cold. When wind strikes the wei-defense, exhaled qi does not come back in; when heat passes into the ying-nutrient, one inhales and it does not go out. Wind damages the skin and hair; heat damages the blood vessels [base: 肺 "lung"]. When wind lodges in the lung, the person coughs, the mouth is dry, there is dyspnea and fullness, the throat is dry but there is no thirst, at times turbid foam is spat, and there are frequent shivering chills. Where heat passes, the blood congeals and stagnates because of it, accumulating and binding into abscess pus, and what is spat is like rice gruel. At the first sprouting it can be rescued; once the pus has formed, the patient dies.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

This says that lung abscess from beginning to end is due entirely to a guest pathogen; compared with atrophy of the lung lobes, with long coughing due to heat, its signs are somewhat more sudden, but the pathogen's progress from outside to inside and from slight to extreme is also gradual. Lung abscess also damages the lung, so there is always cough with counterflow. But at first the signs of abscess are not yet seen, so to distinguish whether it is abscess, pus and blood, or death without cure, one cannot do without the pulse. Is the pulse then immediately rapid and excess? [Such a view] does not know that at first the pulse at the inch opening is originally feeble and rapid. The form of the wind pulse is originally moderate and weak; while the fire is hidden within the lung, only the shadow of the wind pulse is seen outside, feeble; hence "feeble means wind." But the qi is in excess and carries wind, and the heat still shows a rapid picture; hence "rapid means heat." Feeble governs wind; with wind the exterior is deficient and there is spontaneous sweating; hence "feeble means sweating." When the inside is hot, the outside is cold; hence "rapid means aversion to cold."

As it gradually deepens, it tends to pass from the wei-defense to the ying-nutrient. When it strikes the wei-defense, it first reaches the skin and hair and goes to their counterpart, so the wei-defense receives it. But the pathogen is exuberant and does not follow the breathing; so on exhalation the qi merely goes out. The wei-defense has pathogen that does not go out with the exhalation, and the healthy qi at this time can no longer come in to contend with the pathogen. When the wind is depressed into heat and passes into the ying-nutrient aspect, as the qi comes in with inhalation, the pathogenic heat comes in with the inhalation and does not go out. Then the skin and hair receive damage from wind, and the blood vessels receive damage from heat. With wind above, there is cough and dry mouth; with excess lung qi, there is dyspnea and fullness. But the water and fluids conveyed upward gather and do not disperse; so the throat is scorched by fire and dry, while the chest still stores fluid and there is no thirst. Then the wind gradually lodges from its counterpart at the lung's transport point, and with coughing, spitting, and shivering chills, there is tangible congealed stagnation between the lung lobes. One must urgently use the method of draining the lung to drive it down. If one delays further until it is greatly ruined, ruptured, and the lung lobes nearly exhausted, can anything be done? Hence "at the first sprouting it can be rescued; once the pus has formed, the patient dies." "Sprouting" means pus has just begun and is not yet severe.

## Notes

A clause on the pulse and progression of lung abscess.

- At first the cun pulse is feeble and rapid, with wind damaging the wei-defense (sweating, aversion to cold); then heat enters the ying-nutrient and lodges in the lung, with cough, dry mouth, dyspnea and fullness, turbid foam, and shivering chills; finally the blood congeals into pus, and pus like rice gruel is spat out.
- "At the first sprouting it can be rescued; once the pus has formed, the patient dies" shows the importance of early treatment.
- For the base's 熱傷血肺, most other sources read 熱傷血脈 ("heat damages the blood vessels"); 肺 is taken as an error for 脈.
- The base writes 廱 for 癰; it is emended because all other sources read 癰 (see the emendation note).
