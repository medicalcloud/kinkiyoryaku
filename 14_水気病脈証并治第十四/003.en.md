# Clause 3 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

When the pulse at the inch opening is sunken and slippery, there is water qi within; when the face and eyes are greatly swollen with heat, it is called wind water. If one sees that above the eye sockets [base: 目裹] there is slight swelling, as of a silkworm just risen from sleep, the neck vessels pulsate, there is frequent cough, and pressing on the hands and feet leaves a pit that does not rise, it is wind water.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

These two sections take variant forms of wind water and still give them the proper name to show the distinction. The wind water pulse is originally floating; now it is sunken and slippery, meaning there is water qi bound within, which seems to belong to regular water. But the face and eyes are greatly swollen with heat. Above the high vertex only wind can reach, and wind is a yang evil, hence heat. Though the pulse is sunken, one cannot speak of it apart from external wind, so it is still properly named "wind water." Slight silkworm-like swelling of the eye sockets with pulsating neck vessels and cough are signs of regular water. Yet pressing the hands and feet leaves a pit that does not rise. What rises with the hand is water; since it does not rise, we know it is not regular water but qi water. Wind and qi must bind each other, so this too is [properly named wind water].

## Notes

A clause stating that even with a sunken, slippery pulse, facial swelling with heat is wind water, and that swollen eyelids, pulsating neck vessels, cough, and pitting of the hands and feet that does not rebound are also wind water.

- For 目裹, most other sources read 目窠 ("eye socket, eyelid").
- Xu Bin's commentary is cut off at the end.
