# Clause 2 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

The pulse is floating and surging. Floating means wind; surging means qi. When wind and qi contend, if wind is strong there are dormant papules (hives) and the body itches; itching is draining wind, and over time it becomes scabby leprosy. If qi is strong there is water, and it is hard to bend forward or back. When wind and qi strike each other, the body swells greatly; when sweat comes out, there is recovery. Aversion to wind means deficiency; this is wind edema. In one without aversion to wind, whose urination is free, with cold in the upper jiao and much drool in the mouth, this is yellow sweating.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

This section details why wind becomes water and distinguishes it from yellow sweating. When the pulse is floating and surging, floating being wind is reasonable; surging is exuberance of qi. "Wind and qi contend" means wind and qi do not yield to each other. When wind is somewhat stronger, wind governs the disease, invading the blood and producing dormant papules; when fire stirs, there is itching. But the wind can be somewhat drained and dispersed; hence "draining wind." Over time the ying-nutrient qi combines with wind and breeds worms, producing scabby leprosy and pestilential wind, not water.

If qi is strong, the wind is driven by the qi and cannot drain through the skin; going against the pathogen, it forms in the yin aspect, the yin collaterals become diseased, and there is water. "Hard to bend forward or back" is the state of swelling after water has formed. But though the qi is strong, the wind still does not leave; hence "strike each other." Wind and qi reach everywhere, so the body swells greatly — great swelling. When sweat comes out, wind and qi are both drained, so there is recovery. Aversion to wind is the original sign of wind, but if after sweating there is still aversion to wind, one should treat it as deficiency, not as wind; so a phrase is added, "aversion to wind means deficiency," and it is summed up, "this is wind edema," meaning that although water forms through qi, its true origin is wind.

One without aversion to wind has no wind in the exterior. Free urination means it is not binding of the three yin. Further, much drool in the mouth means the qi of water-cold entangles the upper jiao. This is only because yellow sweating is damage by water while sweating, entering inward to the chest and diaphragm; so it is distinguished at once: "cold in the upper jiao and much drool in the mouth." This yellow sweating does not depart from the sense of chest fullness in the earlier yellow sweating signs.

## Notes

A clause describing that when wind and qi contend with a floating, surging pulse, predominant wind produces hives and skin disease, and predominant qi produces water (edema); it distinguishes wind edema from yellow sweating.

- The base reads 汗出則愈; it is emended to 乃愈 because all other sources so read (see the emendation note).
