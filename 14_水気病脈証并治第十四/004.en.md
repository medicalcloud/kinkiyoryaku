# Clause 4 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

In taiyang disease with a floating, tight pulse, by rule the joints should ache, but on the contrary they do not; instead the body is heavy and aching; the person is not thirsty; and when sweat comes out, there is immediate recovery: this is wind water. If there is aversion to cold, this is extreme vacuity, contracted through sweating. If there is thirst without aversion to cold, this is skin water. When the body is swollen and cold, like generalized impediment, with obstruction in the chest, inability to eat, gathering pain instead, and agitation at dusk with inability to sleep, this is yellow sweat. When the pain is in the joints, with cough and panting but no thirst, this is spleen [lung] distension; it looks like swelling; inducing sweating cures it at once. But in all who suffer from these, if there is thirst with diarrhea and frequent urination, sweating must not be induced.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

This section says that among wind water cases some resemble the taiyang pulse but do not show taiyang signs; some resemble it but are in fact skin water; some resemble it but are in fact yellow sweat; and some resemble it but are neither skin water nor yellow sweat but in fact lung distension. In taiyang disease with a floating, tight pulse, by rule the joints should ache; so the earlier description of wind water also says "the external signs are aching joints." Here, on the contrary, they do not ache. Also, taiyang disease is not heavy, yet now with a taiyang cold pulse the body is instead heavy and aching, there is no thirst, and sweating brings immediate recovery; clearly the wind has been softened by water, so it does not ache but is heavy. Wind originally has sweat, and it resolves through spontaneous sweating; so it is properly named "this is wind water." But after sweating there should be no aversion to cold; if there is aversion to cold again, clearly the person has been made vacuous by sweating; hence "this is extreme vacuity, contracted through sweating."

If to the previous signs is added thirst without aversion to cold, the thirst resembles wind water, but without aversion to cold it is not wind water; so it is again distinguished: "this is skin water." But skin water has no body heat, so its definite signs are noted: "the body is swollen and cold, like generalized impediment." Generalized impediment means the skin of the whole body receives evil and does not function; this is the earlier "external signs: puffy swelling that pits when pressed."

If to the previous signs are added obstruction in the chest, inability to eat, gathering pain, and agitation at dusk with inability to sleep, clearly entering water has damaged the heart, and the chest has received evil and is obstructed. The evil is high and hinders eating; the evil gathers and hurts; and the heart is vexed, with agitation at dusk and inability to sleep. These are signs only of yellow sweat, all in the chest; hence "this is yellow sweat."

If with the previous floating, tight pulse the joints still ache, with cough and panting but no thirst, it resembles skin water, but there is not much puffy swelling, and it is not skin water either; hence "this is lung distension." The lung governs qi; receiving evil it coughs; it looks like swelling but is not really swelling. This too is wind soaking into the lung, so it is summed up: "inducing sweating cures it at once," showing that though the signs differ, the treatment may be the same.

The four phrases "in all who suffer from these" mean that although the patterns differ and all seem amenable to sweating, when there is thirst, diarrhea, or free urination, the evil qi has entered inward and cannot be cured by a single sweat; hence "all [must not be made to sweat]."

## Notes

A clause distinguishing water diseases that resemble the taiyang pulse (floating and tight) — wind water, skin water, yellow sweat, and lung distension — all curable by sweating, but prohibiting sweating in those with thirst, diarrhea, or frequent urination.

- For 脾 in 此為脾脹 ("spleen distension"), icntcm reads 肺 ("lung"), and Xu Bin's edition also suspects an error for 肺. Since the content matches the lung-distension clauses (cough, ascending qi, panting) of the earlier chapter on lung atrophy and lung abscess, the translation notes "lung."
- Interpretations differ on where "the body is swollen and cold, like generalized impediment" belongs; Xu Bin takes it as supplementing the signs of skin water.
- The base reads 及不疼; it is emended to 反不疼 because all other sources so read (see the emendation note).
