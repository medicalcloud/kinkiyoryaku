# Clause 29 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

The master said: The pulse at the inch opening is slow and hesitant. Slow means cold; hesitant means insufficient blood. The anterior tibial (Fuyang) pulse is feeble and slow. Feeble means qi; slow means cold. With cold and insufficient qi, the hands and feet are counterflow cold; with counterflow cold of the hands and feet, the ying-nutrient and wei-defense are not free; when the ying-nutrient and wei-defense are not free, the abdomen is full and the ribsides rumble, chasing each other, the qi turns to the bladder, and ying-nutrient and wei-defense are both exhausted. When yang qi does not flow, the body is cold; when yin qi does not flow, the bones ache. If yang flows first, there is aversion to cold; if yin flows first, there is impediment and numbness. When yin and yang combine, the qi moves; when the great qi turns once, the qi disperses. If excess, there is flatus; if deficient, enuresis. This is called the qi aspect.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

This section is not the yellow sweating pattern. Because the pulse of yellow sweating is slow, the ying-nutrient and wei-defense above and below do not connect, and over time chest pain, lumbar and hip pain, and aching heaviness arise from the qi aspect, it extends by analogy to deficiency-cold patterns in which insufficiency of qi and blood originates in the qi aspect, detailing how the disease is contracted, how it is cured, and how it is the same and different, to enlighten people in identifying patterns. The cun pulse governs ying-nutrient and wei-defense; it is slow and hesitant: slow is deficient yang, cold; hesitant is deficient yin, insufficient blood. The anterior tibial (Fuyang) pulse governs spleen and stomach; feeble means the stomach's original qi has declined, and when it declines, deficient qi instead forms stuffiness; hence "feeble means qi." Slow too is cold. Taken together, it is cold, qi, and insufficient blood — great deficiency of qi and blood with cold added.

The hands and feet are the root of all yang; when the genuine qi does not reach them, they are counterflow cold. Yang qi rises from the limbs to penetrate the whole body and regulate the ying-nutrient and wei-defense; with counterflow cold, the ying-nutrient and wei-defense are not free. When not free, the genuine qi is lacking and deficient qi overflows sideways, seeming instead to be surplus; so the abdomen is full, the ribsides rumble chasing each other, the qi turns, and the bladder and the ying-nutrient and wei-defense, with no true yang to command them, are all fatigued and depleted; hence "both exhausted." Then when the Taiyang of the bladder has no master, the yang qi does not flow and the body is cold; when the yin qi of the ying-nutrient and wei-defense is greatly deficient, the yin qi does not flow and the bones ache. If the pathway of the qi of food opens and the yang qi flows first, the yang qi of the whole body is still obstructed, and there is aversion to cold. If the nourishment of food moistens and the yin qi flows first, the yin qi of the whole body is still withered, and there is impediment and numbness. In all, yin and yang are estranged, blocked, and form stuffiness. Is the way to overturn the stuffiness anything other than regulating the origin to accomplish the functions of initiating and generating? Hence "when yin and yang combine, the qi moves; when the great qi turns once, the qi disperses." This is the principle of *qian* being vigorous, with origination, success, benefit, and correctness. When the qi is blocked, the excess pass flatus, the pathogen draining through the stool, and the deficient have enuresis, the pathogen draining through the urine. Though its source is also insufficient blood, how the disease forms and disperses is governed by the one qi; hence "qi aspect."

Discussion: Why did Zhongjing, after discussing generalized edema, conclude with a blood aspect, and after discussing yellow sweating, conclude with a qi aspect? Generalized edema is the kidney receiving pathogen and arising in the lower jiao, where blood is the master of function; so discussing generalized edema he extended it to blocked menstrual blood. Yellow sweating is the heart receiving pathogen and arising in the upper jiao, where qi is the master of function; so from yellow sweating he extended it to the great qi not turning. Only the qi and blood, yin and yang of the upper and lower jiao differ. This is why Zhongjing in treating yellow sweating makes gui the sovereign for its transformation of qi, in treating generalized edema makes mahuang (mao) the sovereign for its entry into the ying-nutrient, and for stony edema makes fuzi (bushi) the mainstay for its enriching of yin. If one examines the order of his statements, is not the intent of his formulas clear?

## Notes

A clause using the cun and Fuyang pulses to describe "qi aspect" disease, in which cold and insufficiency of qi and blood produce cold limbs, abdominal fullness, rumbling, a cold body, bone pain, aversion to cold, and numbness.

- It states that the key to treatment is harmonizing yin and yang so the qi moves: "when yin and yang combine and the great qi turns once, the qi disperses."
- It pairs with the "blood aspect" of unit 19.
- The base lacks 気 after 陽 and 陰 before 気, and reads 骨痛; these are emended to 陽気, 陰気, and 骨疼 because all other sources so read (see the emendation notes).
