# Clause 31 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Diarrhea where the cun pulse is instead floating and rapid, and the chi position is hesitant of itself: there will surely be passing of pus and blood.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

The previous section already said, "Diarrhea with a feeble, weak, and rapid pulse is about to stop by itself; even with fever there is no death." These six clauses, based on that idea, say that pulse and signs may vary, but the principle of welcoming the internal pathogen's exit outward is one and the same — only when it turns to heat, blood will surely be seen. Thus: diarrhea is originally visiting cold damaging the interior; unless it is a yin pattern from direct strike, yin and yang will contend with each other. If yin wins, it is hard to heal; if yang wins, it is easy to heal. Suppose there is slight heat: the pathogen has come out to the exterior. Thirst means the yang in the chest has won. And if the pulse is weak, little pathogenic qi remains inside. Even without treatment, when the pathogen goes, the healthy qi recovers by itself; so it is allowed to heal by itself, and there is no need to seek merit and create trouble.

If there is already slight heat and the pulse is not weak but rapid, rapid is also yang winning; and with sweating, the heat drains outward. So it is again allowed to heal by itself. If tightness accompanies the rapid pulse, the pathogenic cold is still firm, and it has not yet resolved.

If a rapid pulse and thirst are seen together, this too is yang winning, so it is allowed to heal by itself. If it does not get better, the cold has already retreated but the disease has not, and cold should not be blamed; rather, much heat will instead stir the blood; hence "there will surely be passing of pus and blood, because there is heat."

If there is fever and sweating as above and the pulse is also wiry, an interior sign is seen; but wiry is a yang pulse, so yang has won, and when yang wins there is recovery.

Then there is diarrhea with unceasing passing of gas. This is stagnant and disordered qi, outside the matter of cold and heat; so one simply disinhibits the urine. When urine flows, qi transforms; when qi transforms, it is not disordered.

If diarrhea truly belongs to cold, the pulse should be deep and slow; if it is instead floating and rapid, yang has evidently won. And if the chi position is rough of itself, roughness is yang pathogen entering yin; this too is much heat; hence "there will surely be passing of pus and blood."

Discussion: the causes of diarrhea are many, and one must examine them in detail. There is passing of intestinal filth from heat damage, very foul-smelling and yellow. There is heat-assisted diarrhea from mistaken purgation, always with heat below the navel or heat at the anus. There is diarrhea with bound dry stool, always with delirium. There is straight discharge of water: food damage stagnates the qi in the intestines so that separation [of clear and turbid] fails in its duty. There is discharge of clear water, pure green-blue, always with pain below the heart and dry mouth: this is Shaoyin disease with visiting heat attacking the liver and kidney inside, a pattern that should be purged most urgently. There is Shaoyin disease with desire to vomit without vomiting, heart vexation, and only wanting to sleep, followed after five or six days by spontaneous diarrhea with thirst; it belongs to Shaoyin, and white urine makes this all the more certain, since cold in the lower jiao cannot control water and makes it white. There is habitual diarrhea at dawn: this is kidney diarrhea. There is diarrhea lasting several years: this is called water and earth transforming together, and is spleen diarrhea. There is diarrhea that comes and goes: this is damp diarrhea, always with slight distension. There is intermittent diarrhea after which one feels better: this is retained fluid diarrhea. There is diarrhea from phlegm congesting the lung qi and making the large intestine deficient, always with slippery pulses at both cun positions. There is undigested grain: this is swill diarrhea from wind damage. There is sloppy stool: this is damp qi. There is duck-like sloppy stool, clear water with thready bits like duck droppings: this is lung deficiency or cold in the large intestine. And there is stool that is neither water, nor undigested grain, nor intestinal filth, but simply not yellow, not very foul, loose and not solid: this is diarrhea with clear grain. The several passages of the main text are precisely this so-called diarrhea with clear grain. "Clear grain" means the food has been digested but is not solid; compared with the sloppy stool of impending recovery, it has water mixed in.

## Notes

A clause stating that diarrhea with a floating, rapid cun pulse and a hesitant chi pulse will lead to stools with pus and blood. The same clause appears in the Jueyin chapter of the *Shanghan lun* (corresponding to clause 363).

- Xu Bin discusses together the six clauses from unit 26 to this unit, taking all as cases of yang prevailing and pathogen about to exit, with blood appearing when it turns to heat. In the latter part he lists the causes and types of diarrhea.
- For 清, Wikisource (*Jingui yaolüe*) and Xu Bin's text read 圊.
