# Clause 11 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

The dead-zang organ [pulse] of the heart: superficially it is in excess like hemp seeds or beans; on pressure it becomes ever more agitated and rapid; the patient dies.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

The heart is the sovereign fire and master of the five zang organs, originally governing by non-action. Wind is a yang pathogen; when it combines with [the heart], there is fever. "Feather-warm" describes something arising suddenly and evenly, as in the *Analects*' "when it first begins, it is harmonious (翕如)." Vigorous fire eats qi, so one cannot rise. Hunger is fire gnawing. Vomiting as soon as one eats is because pathogenic heat does not tolerate grain. The *Neijing* says, "All vomiting and sourness belong to heat." But all this is pathogenic wind drawing in pathogenic fire to cause trouble, because wind belongs to yang pathogen.

If it is cold, it is yin pathogen; when it binds the outside, fire gathers within; hence the feeling as if one had eaten garlic, meaning something like pungency but not pain. When severe, the pathogen is exuberant, so outwardly it attacks the back with pain and inwardly attacks the heart with pain. "Penetrate" means they correspond. The pathogen occupies the pathways of qi, and the healthy qi is instead made its servant, so the pains seem to correspond. "Like being gnawed by worms" means it continues without stopping. If the pulse is floating, the pathogen has not yet bound, so vomiting can bring recovery.

In one whose heart is damaged, either a guest pathogen has damaged the spirit light within, or the healthy qi has not yet recovered; so even if the exterior pathogen is gone, at the first fatigue the ministerial fire combines with it, the true yin does not hold, and heart fire flames upward, so the head and face turn red. When the genuine qi of the zang organ has followed the fire upward, the yin below has no yang to lift it, so the lower body is heavy. The yang guarding the exterior cannot enter to communicate with the heart, so there is fever. A person's qi and blood nourish each other; when the heart is deficient and cannot move its heat, there is pain and vexation. When the visceral qi does not interact and drums within in depression, there is throbbing at the umbilicus. The pulse is wiry: wiry is diminution, the healthy qi bound and deficient. So it concludes, "caused by damage to the heart zang organ."

The heart pulse is originally like *langgan* jade. Excess like hemp seeds or beans means hard; appearing in the floating pulse means the flames are high. Becoming ever more agitated and rapid on pressure, the momentum is like a fire at its height; the yin qi has already been cut off, so the patient dies.

Discussion: What generates the myriad things is fire, and what kills them is also fire. The substance of fire is heat; its function is warmth. So in a cauldron it cooks to nourish, while a prairie fire scorches and withers. The above signs are the right being made the servant of the pathogen, so that heart fire loses its function of gentle yang warmth, and everything in the body that relies on yang for warmth changes like this. This details the internal picture of the heart struck by wind. What the *Neijing* says — "When the heart is struck by wind, there is much sweating, aversion to wind, parching, frequent anger and alarm; when severe, speech is not fluent; the diagnosis is at the mouth, the color black" — and the *Qianjin*'s "the diagnosis is at the lips, the color red" speak of the external picture of the heart struck by wind.

## Notes

A clause on the dead-zang organ pulse of the heart. A pulse hard and excess like hemp seeds or beans superficially, and ever more agitated and rapid on pressure, indicates exhaustion of the heart's genuine qi and death.

- Xu Bin's commentary explains the heart clauses of units 8 through 11 together.
