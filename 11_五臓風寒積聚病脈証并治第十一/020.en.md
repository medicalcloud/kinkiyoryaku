# Clause 20 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Question: Among diseases there are accumulations (*ji*), gatherings (*ju*), and grain qi (*gu qi*); what do these mean? The master said: Accumulations are diseases of the viscera; they never move. Gatherings are diseases of the bowels; they occur in attacks at times, the pain shifting about; they can be treated. Grain qi is pain below the ribs that is relieved by pressing but recurs; this is grain qi.

The general rule for accumulations: when the pulse comes fine and clings to the bone, it is an accumulation. At the inch opening, the accumulation is in the chest; slightly beyond the inch opening, it is in the throat; at the bar, it is beside the umbilicus; above the bar, it is below the heart; slightly below the bar, it is in the lesser abdomen; at the cubit, it is at Qi Thoroughfare. If the pulse appears on the left, the accumulation is on the left; on the right, it is on the right; on both, it is in the center. In each case treat according to the region.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

The ancients' names always have meaning. All are pains within the triple burner, yet some are called accumulations, some gatherings, and some grain qi. An accumulation (*ji*) is a trace (*ji*), a foul qi belonging to yin. The viscera belong to yin; two yins combining, it does not move. "Not moving" means there is a fixed place of pain without change. A gathering is like goods gathering by chance in a market, a disease qi belonging to yang. The bowels belong to yang, so they are paired. Being yang, it does not congeal like yin; so when cold qi is felt it breaks out, otherwise it stops — what is called "at times." Having no fixed attachment, the pain has no constant place; hence "the pain shifting about." Its root is not deep, so compared with accumulation it can be treated.

The *gu* of grain qi is grain, that is, the qi of food. Food damages the thick-earth qi of taiyin and represses the liver qi, so the pain is below the ribs. The disease is not due to the viscera and bowels, so pressing relieves it. But though the disease qi is light, pressing cannot cut off its source, so it recurs. If the central qi is strong, it recovers by itself without treatment. Being the lightest disease, it is not even said to be treatable.

Discussion: These accumulations are not of the concretion-conglomeration kind, nor necessarily tangible lodged accumulations. All things under heaven arise from nothing; that is, qi binds in yin, and yin is sticky. The later text's "the accumulation is in the throat" shows it is binding in yin; otherwise how could the throat contain a tangible thing?

Accumulation diseases are hard and long-standing and difficult to treat, so the pulse and location must be detailed to show the method of pattern identification. Accumulations belong to yin; fine, small, and sunken is the image of yin. Hence "the general rule for accumulations: the pulse comes fine." The nutritive qi binds, and binding produces accumulation. "Clings to the bone" describes extreme sinking; it does not mean the disease is in the bone. The inch opening governs the upper burner, and the chest is the upper burner; hence "in the chest." "Slightly" means a little; a little beyond the inch opening, above the chest is the throat; hence "in the throat," like throat impediment. The bar governs the middle burner, whose governing point is beside the umbilicus; hence "beside the umbilicus." Above the bar is the bottom of the upper burner and top of the middle burner; hence "below the heart." Slightly below the bar is the lower burner, governed by the lesser abdomen; hence "in the lesser abdomen." Qi Thoroughfare is near the pubic hairline on the inner thighs, and its qi communicates with the lower burner; hence "at the cubit, the accumulation is at Qi Thoroughfare." "If the pulse appears on the left, the accumulation is on the left" means when the pulse appears at the left hand, the accumulation is on the left inside; likewise for the right. "On both" means seen at both hands; an accumulation cannot straddle both sides, so clearly the central qi responds on both sides; hence "in the center." Since the location varies, the treatment differs; hence "in each case [treat according to] its [region]."

## Notes

A clause distinguishing accumulations (visceral diseases, immovable masses), gatherings (bowel diseases, occurring at times with shifting pain), and grain qi (pain below the ribs relieved by pressure but recurring), and describing how to locate an accumulation by where the pulse appears.

- 㯏気 is read 䅽気 or 馨気 in other sources and understood as "grain qi."
- The location of the accumulation is judged by where the pulse appears: inch opening (chest), beyond the inch (throat), bar (beside the umbilicus), above the bar (below the heart), below the bar (lesser abdomen), cubit (Qi Thoroughfare).
- The base reads 病者積 and 小腹; these are emended to 病有積 and 少腹 because all other sources so read (see the emendation notes).
