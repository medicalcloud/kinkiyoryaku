# Clause 7 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Liver distension: the person always wants to have his chest trodden upon (pressed). Before the distress comes on, he only wants to drink hot things. Xuanfuhua tang (Senpukukato; Inula Flower Decoction) governs it (Your servants [Lin] Yi and others, collating the various editions, [found] the Xuanfuhua tang formula the same in all [other sources: missing in all]).

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

Liver distension is like something sticking and not flowing, unlike sudden contraction of wind-cold with signs appearing in season. The qi of the disease does not move, so one always wants to press the chest; to press means to press and rub. Liking hot drinks before the distress comes on is because an immovable pathogen lies hidden within; meeting heat it disperses somewhat, and as the qi cools it congeals further; so one likes to pour hot drinks onto it. But when great distress comes on, the qi of the disease breaks out as heat, which hot drinks cannot overcome. Hence "before the distress comes on."

Xuanfuhua tang is the later Xuanfuhua (senpukuka) with scallion and a little xinjiang (new red dye) added. Xuanfuhua is salty and warm, and can soften hardness and bring down water; so Hu Qia used it for phlegm-fluid retention in both ribsides with distension and fullness, and Zhongjing, for stuffiness and hardness below the heart with unceasing belching after cold has been purged, has the seven-ingredient Xuanfu Daizhe tang (Senpukutaishato). Master Kou says it is cold and disinhibits the large intestine and should not be used where deficiency is involved; but seeing that Zhongjing treats miscarriage and metrostaxis with deficiency and cold contending and a wiry, hollow pulse with it, we know xuanfuhua moves water, brings down qi, and frees the blood vessels. It must not be overused, but where disease is in both ribsides and below the heart, hard, congealed, and immovable, nothing but this will work even if there is deficiency. Since [Lin] Yi and the others did not annotate its formula, it is omitted.

Discussion: For the earlier wind and cold [clauses] no formulas are given; only here is a formula set out. This is because liver distension is a distinct disease gradually developing from wind and cold, not the proper disease of wind attack.

## Notes

A clause using Xuanfuhua tang for liver distension (qi and blood of the liver stuck and stagnant in the chest and ribsides), in which the patient wants the chest pressed and, before attacks, wants hot drinks.

- "Have his chest trodden upon" is taken to mean wanting to press or pound the chest. Xu Bin's edition suggests it may be an error for 搯 ("press").
- The Xuanfuhua tang formula is not in this chapter. The later chapter on miscellaneous diseases of women has a formula "xuanfuhua 3 liang, scallion 14 stalks, a little xinjiang," which Daizhige inserts here.
- For 皆同 ("the same in all") in the interlinear note, icntcm reads 皆闕 ("missing in all"), which fits the context better; Xu Bin also says it is omitted because Lin Yi and others did not annotate it.
- The base reads 当欲蹈; it is emended to 常 because all other sources so read (see the emendation note).
