# Clause 3 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

The dead-viscus [pulse] of the lung: superficially it is vacuous; on pressure it is weak like a scallion leaf; if there is no root below, the patient dies.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

The large intestine governs the fluids, and lung and large intestine are exterior and interior to each other. When the lung receives evil, the qi of the large intestine does not transform, so the mouth is dry. The lung is the master of qi; the evil contends with its breathing, so there is panting; this is replete panting. The lung governs the qi of the whole body; receiving evil, it cannot be as vigorous as usual, so there is swaying and heaviness. Swaying is like being on a cart or boat, unable to control oneself. Heaviness is because the qi in the flesh stagnates and does not move freely. When evil qi is replete, clear qi stagnates, so the clear yang does not rise and the head is veiled. Inside and outside all rely on qi to flow. The lung has received evil, inside and outside are all congested; when congested, the outside swells and the inside distends. Cold is a yin evil, and yin governs turbidity, so turbid matter or snivel is spat out. When turbid matter is spat out, the diaphragm area too turns hot, but its root is cold. The lung pulse is originally floating and rough. If vacuous, the original qi is deficient and weak. A scallion is hollow and soft; feeling like a scallion leaf on pressure means the yang above does not descend to the yin. In severe cases, with no root below, the original qi has entirely deserted, so the patient dies.

Discussion: All the above signs speak of the lung itself receiving disease. Then the damage is to the qi, and everything in the body that relies on qi for its normal functioning produces various changed signs like these. This details the internal picture of the lung struck by wind and cold. What the *Neijing* says — "The appearance of lung wind: much sweating, aversion to wind, occasional cough, better by day and worse in the evening; the diagnosis is above the eyebrows, the color white" — speaks of the external picture of the lung affected by exterior evil. Note: the discussion of water qi says, "When the stomach is vacuous, there is swelling and distension." Here, discussing wind-strike of the lung, swelling and distension are also mentioned. The spleen qi disperses essence upward to return to the lung; if the lung evil is heavy and does not accept the conveyance, the spleen cannot extend itself, and the stomach qi also stagnates; so it too can produce swelling and distension. But the lung's swelling is due to wind, so compared with the swelling of stomach vacuity it is repletion within vacuity.

## Notes

A clause on the dead-viscus pulse of the lung. In this chapter, for each of the five viscera, the pulse indicating that the true qi of that viscus is exhausted is given as the "dead viscus."

- A pulse vacuous superficially, hollow like a scallion leaf on pressure, and rootless below (deep) means the original qi of the lung has deserted, and the patient dies.
- Xu Bin's commentary explains the lung clauses of units 1 through 3 together.
