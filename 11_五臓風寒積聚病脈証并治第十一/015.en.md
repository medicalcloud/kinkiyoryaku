# Clause 15 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

The instep yang pulse is floating and rough. Floating means the stomach qi is strong; rough means urination is frequent. When floating and rough contend, the stool becomes hard, and the spleen is constrained. Maziren wan (Hemp Seed Pill) governs it.

Maziren wan formula

Maziren (hemp seed) 2 sheng; shaoyao half a jin; zhishi 1 jin; dahuang 1 jin; houpo 1 chi; xingren 1 sheng.

Powder the six ingredients above and make refined-honey pills the size of firmiana seeds; take ten pills with a drink, three times a day, until there is an effect.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

The instep yang is the pulse of spleen and stomach. The spleen originally has dry heat; external evil enters and makes it worse, and when worse the qi increases, so the pulse is floating; floating means yang qi is strong. Rough means the yin qi has no surplus, so urination is frequent and the stool hard. So maziren moistens it, shaoyao is included to nourish yin, dahuang brings down heat, zhishi expels the tangible, houpo disperses bound qi, xingren disinhibits the large intestine, and honey is added; then the qi is cooled and the blood too is cooled, and the dry heat vanishes. A pill is used rather than a decoction to take gentleness in opening the binding, not wishing to damage the original qi suddenly. One should know that reaching spleen constraint is always due to insufficient original qi. But renshen and huangqi are not used for fear that the qi, supplemented, would increase the heat.

Discussion: Zhongjing, discussing joint-running, says, "The instep yang pulse is floating and slippery; slippery means grain qi is replete; floating means sweat comes out spontaneously." Discussing wasting thirst he says, "The instep yang pulse is floating and rapid; floating is qi; rapid is dispersing grain and great hardness; when qi is exuberant, urination is frequent; when urination is frequent, [the stool] is hard; when hardness and rapidity contend, there is wasting thirst." Discussing water swelling he says, "The instep yang pulse is floating and rapid; the floating pulse is heat; the rapid pulse is stopping; when heat and stopping contend, it is called hidden." Discussing grain jaundice he says, "The instep yang pulse is tight and rapid; rapid is heat, and heat disperses grain; tight is cold, and eating causes fullness." Discussing stomach reflux he says, "The instep yang pulse is floating and rough; floating damages the spleen, and when the spleen is damaged it does not grind; eaten in the morning, vomited in the evening; eaten in the evening, vomited in the morning." Here, discussing spleen constraint, he says, "The instep yang pulse is floating and rough; floating [means the stomach qi is strong] ..."

... we know that these several patterns all concern the spleen and stomach, all with strong yang and weak yin. When weak, evil lodges there; the original qi cannot move and joins with yang heat. So carrying wind-damp, there is joint-running pain with sweating: pain and sweating are the substance of wind-damp, but their origin is disharmony of central earth, so the qi is too feeble to overcome the evil in the flesh. Carrying qi, heat accumulates in the spleen yin, producing wasting thirst; the heat binds hard as stone, and even water cannot relieve it. When water and qi obstruct each other, there is water swelling: the water is driven by the qi and cannot moisten and descend, but goes over the forehead (flows backward). When food accumulation and cold-damp steam each other, there is grain jaundice. When the spleen yin is depleted, it cannot grind food, producing stomach reflux. When guest wind changes things, the stomach is strong and the spleen constrained. But floating and rapid are both qi heat; slippery is surplus, rough is consumption of yin. So the spleen-constraint pill centers on moistening dryness, while stomach reflux is at once said to be "difficult to treat"; here there is a slight distinction.

Discussing evil received in the blood aspect, with cold and water contending, he says, "When the instep yang pulse is hidden, water and grain are not transformed; when the spleen qi declines there is duck-stool diarrhea; when the stomach qi declines the body swells." Discussing cold in the qi aspect with hardness below the heart as large as a plate, he says, "The instep yang pulse is faint and slow; faint is qi; slow is cold; when the cold qi is insufficient, the hands and feet are counterflow cold; with counterflow cold, the nutritive and defensive are not free; when not free, the abdomen is full and the ribsides rumble, chasing each other as the qi turns." Discussing abdominal fullness he says, "When the instep yang pulse is faint and wiry, by rule there should be abdominal fullness." All the above say that when the spleen and stomach are vacuous and cold, there are swelling, fullness, duck-stool diarrhea, and abdominal rumbling, and the pulse does not go beyond wiry, hidden, slow, and faint. Pattern identification by the instep yang is the clearest and most pertinent. It is a pity that people today omit it and do not discuss it; no wonder Zhongjing reproached those who "press the hand but do not reach the foot."

... adding, until there is an effect.

## Notes

A clause using Maziren wan when strong stomach heat constrains the spleen's fluids (spleen constraint), with frequent urination and hard stool. The same clause appears in the yangming chapter of the *Shanghan lun* (corresponding to clause 247).

- Maziren wan is Xiao Chengqi tang (dahuang, zhishi, houpo) plus maziren, xingren, and shaoyao, made into honey pills; it moistens the intestines and frees them gently.
- Xu Bin's "Discussion" gathers the clauses of the *Jingui* that identify patterns by the instep yang pulse. Some of the quotations in the commentary are cut off.
- The base reads 胸気強 ("chest qi is strong"); it is emended to 胃気強 because all other sources so read (see the emendation note).
