# Clause 5 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Question: When the cun kou pulse is floating, feeble, and hesitant, there should then [most other sources: "by rule"] be loss of blood or sweating. If there is no sweating, what is it? Answer: If there is a sore on the body, it is because one was injured by a knife or axe and lost blood.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

This clause details why there is no sweating where there should be. The cun kou is yang; floating seems like yang exuberance, but feebleness means yang is feeble, so this floating is fire exuberance, not yang exuberance. Floating, feeble, and hesitant means blood is depleted and yin is hot; when yin is hot, the blood is assailed by fire and fluids are lost through heat; so there should be loss of blood or sweating. But when this pulse is seen and there is instead no sweating, one suspects the floating is not due to blood loss. Seeing a scar on the body and knowing it was a knife or axe injury, blood has already been lost earlier. One whose blood has been robbed has no sweat, so no sweat comes out. No formula is given because the emphasis is on distinguishing pulse and sweat, not on discussing treatment.

## Notes

A clause stating that a floating, feeble, and hesitant cun kou pulse should be accompanied by blood loss or sweating, and the absence of sweat is because blood has already been lost through a knife wound or the like.

- For 然 ("then"), Wikisource (*Jingui yaolüe*), Daizhige, and Xu Bin's text read 法 ("by rule").
