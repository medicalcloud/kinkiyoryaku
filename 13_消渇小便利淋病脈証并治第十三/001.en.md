# Clause 1 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Jueyin disease: wasting thirst, qi surging up into the heart, pain and heat in the heart, hunger [base: 熱 "heat"] without desire to eat, vomiting as soon as one eats, and if purged, [diarrhea] that will not stop.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

The seven characters "jueyin disease: wasting thirst" are the great source of wasting-thirst disease. Wasting thirst means readily dispersing [food] and great thirst. There may be only unceasing thirst, or eating much with thirst, or thirst with, on the contrary, copious urination; later people therefore distinguished upper, middle, and lower wasting. They did not know that although upper, middle, and lower seem to differ, the source of the disease always belongs to jueyin. The liver vessel is jueyin, and jueyin is the organ of wind-wood, of a kind with wind, so all wind diseases must strike the liver first. But wind moves readily and changes often, so it may be in the channels, in the blood vessels, or in the flesh, each different. When it is depressed in the organ itself, the liver obtains the evil and becomes replete, and then overwhelms what it overcomes, so yangming receives it, and overwhelms what generates it, so foot shaoyin receives it. Thus there may be one-sided predominance in upper, middle, or lower, with slightly different signs, but all are wasting thirst, all due to jueyin wind depressed and fire blazing. Hence "jueyin disease: wasting thirst." The *Neijing* also has the term "wind wasting"; that wasting must be spoken of together with wind has this meaning.

When the liver is replete with evil, wood qi likes to extend upward, so qi surges up into the heart; the heart is pressed by the evil, so it hurts and is hot. When the liver obtains heat and becomes dry, the child steals the mother's qi, so the kidney also falls ill; hence hunger without desire to eat. Vomiting as soon as one eats is because the upper part receives the surging of the evil qi, and the liver governs retching counterflow. If purged, it will not stop, because the disease is not due to stomach repletion, yet the stomach is attacked, so it still does not stop.

Discussion: The *Neijing* says, "When the two yang are bound, it is called wasting," whereas here only jueyin is central; they seem to differ. But [such a view] does not know that the evil qi soaks in, the disease deepens into the intestines and stomach, and the qi gathers without dispersing; hence "bound." If it prevents the qi of intestines and stomach from moving vigorously and produces the three wastings, then jueyin is truly the root of the disease. If the disease were solely in the intestines and stomach, purging would hit the disease and the wasting thirst should always stop. Moreover, eating much with unceasing hunger is middle wasting, yet here it says "hunger without desire to eat"; so we know that in wasting-thirst disease there are also those who do not want to eat. Those who can eat and are thirsty should be treated with emphasis on the two yang; those who drink one and urinate two, with emphasis on kidney vacuity; and those who cannot eat and have surging qi, with emphasis on jueyin. This too is a fine distinction at the bedside.

## Notes

The opening clause of the chapter on wasting thirst, inhibited urination, and strangury. As jueyin disease it lists wasting thirst (intense thirst), surging qi, pain and heat in the chest, poor appetite, vomiting, and diarrhea. It is nearly the same as the outline clause of jueyin disease in the *Shanghan lun* (corresponding to clause 326).

- For 熱 in 熱而不欲食, all other sources read 飢 or 饑 ("hunger"), and the *Shanghan lun* also reads 饑而不欲食. The base appears to have miswritten the character; it has been emended to 飢 (see the emendation notes).
- Against the later division into upper, middle, and lower wasting, Xu Bin argues that the root of wasting thirst lies in jueyin.
