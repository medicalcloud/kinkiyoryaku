# Clause 11 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Question: The cun pulse is deep, large, and slippery. Deep indicates excess; slippery indicates qi. Excess and qi contend with each other; if blood and qi enter the zang organs, death follows at once; if they enter the bowels, recovery follows at once. This is called sudden syncope. What does this mean? The master said: If the lips and mouth are green-blue and the body is cold, it has entered the zang organs, and death follows at once. If the body is warm and sweat comes out by itself, it has entered the bowels, and recovery follows at once.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

The cun pulse is the position of the heart and lung, where spirit-qi resides. Being deep rather than floating means the pathogen is in excess. Being large and also slippery is the qi of disease. The qi of the disease pathogen contends with blood and qi, stirs and damages the spirit light, and the disease comes on suddenly; so there is no doubt that it is called "sudden syncope."

But it says, "entering the zang organs means death, entering the bowels means recovery." Since the pulse is already deep, and yet zang-fu organs are further distinguished, it is doubtful what is meant. [Such doubt] fails to see that this belongs to the category of wind attack. Wind likes to go to the liver and restrain the spleen, so the pathogen combines with the spleen and the lips and mouth turn green-blue; yang qi does not flow, so the body is cold. "Entering the zang organs" means transmission inward. If the body is warm and sweat comes out, the pathogen does not run inward but outward, and outside it disperses. "Entering the bowels" means coming out.

## Notes

A clause on the prognosis of "sudden syncope," a sudden loss of consciousness and collapse.

- It arises when the cun pulse is deep, large, and slippery, and excess (pathogen) contends with qi.
- Green-blue lips and a cold body mean "entering the zang organs" and death; a warm body with sweating means "entering the bowels" and recovery. Zang-fu organs here are often understood to express the depth of the disease (see also the next clause).
- The base reads 伝 for 搏 ("contend"); it is emended because all other sources read 搏 (see the emendation note).
