# Clause 11 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Question: The inch pulse is sunken, large, and slippery. Sunken indicates repletion; slippery indicates qi. Repletion and qi contend with each other; if blood and qi enter the viscera, death follows at once; if they enter the bowels, recovery follows at once. This is called sudden reversal. What does this mean? The master said: If the lips and mouth are green-blue and the body is cold, it has entered the viscera, and death follows at once. If the body is warm and sweat comes out by itself, it has entered the bowels, and recovery follows at once.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

The inch pulse is the position of the heart and lung, where spirit-qi resides. Being sunken rather than floating means the evil is replete. Being large and also slippery is the qi of disease. The qi of the disease evil contends with blood and qi, stirs and damages the spirit light, and the disease comes on suddenly; so there is no doubt that it is called "sudden reversal."

But it says, "entering the viscera means death, entering the bowels means recovery." Since the pulse is already sunken, and yet viscera and bowels are further distinguished, it is doubtful what is meant. [Such doubt] fails to see that this belongs to the category of wind-strike. Wind likes to go to the liver and restrain the spleen, so the evil combines with the spleen and the lips and mouth turn green-blue; yang qi does not flow, so the body is cold. "Entering the viscera" means transmission inward. If the body is warm and sweat comes out, the evil does not run inward but outward, and outside it disperses. "Entering the bowels" means coming out.

## Notes

A clause on the prognosis of "sudden reversal," a sudden loss of consciousness and collapse.

- It arises when the inch pulse is sunken, large, and slippery, and repletion (evil) contends with qi.
- Green-blue lips and a cold body mean "entering the viscera" and death; a warm body with sweating means "entering the bowels" and recovery. Viscera and bowels here are often understood to express the depth of the disease (see also the next clause).
- The base reads 伝 for 搏 ("contend"); it is emended because all other sources read 搏 (see the emendation note).
