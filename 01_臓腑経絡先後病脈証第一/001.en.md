# Clause 1 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Question: The superior physician treats what is not yet ill. What does this mean? The master said: To treat what is not yet ill means that when one sees a disease of the liver, one knows that the liver [disease] will pass to the spleen, and one should first make the spleen replete. In the [last eighteen days of each of the] four seasons the spleen is flourishing and does not receive evil, so one should not supplement it [then]. The average physician does not understand transmission; seeing a disease of the liver, he does not know to make the spleen replete and treats only the liver.

A disease of the liver is supplemented with sour, assisted with scorched bitter, and benefited with sweet-flavored medicinals to regulate it. Sour enters the liver, scorched bitter enters the heart, sweet enters the spleen. The spleen can damage the kidney; when kidney qi is weak, water does not move; when water does not move, the qi of heart fire is exuberant; when the qi of heart fire is exuberant, it damages the lung; when the lung is damaged, metal qi does not move; when metal qi does not move, liver qi is exuberant. Therefore, if the spleen is made replete, the liver recovers by itself. This is the essential subtlety of supplementing the spleen to treat the liver. When the liver is vacuous, use this method; when it is replete, it is not to be used. The classic says: "Do not make vacuity more vacuous or repletion more replete; supplement insufficiency and reduce surplus." This is its meaning. The other viscera follow this.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

In medicine there are major key points that cannot be confined to a single disease. Zhongjing particularly set out several dozen items in the first chapter to establish the methods of treatment. This clause discusses the principle of the mutual restraint of the five phases: [disease] necessarily passes on in sequence, so one must prepare in advance to prevent its transmission.

The question is: since of old it has been said that "the superior physician treats what is not yet ill," does he really treat before there is any illness at all? [The answer is that] by the principle of mutual restraint, [disease] always passes to what it overcomes. For example, seeing a disease of the liver, since liver wood overcomes spleen earth, one knows it will surely pass to the spleen and works first to make the spleen replete. To make the spleen replete before it is ill is what is called treating what is not yet ill. But in the four seasonal periods earth is flourishing, and when flourishing it does not receive evil, so one should not supplement it, for fear of making repletion more replete. The average physician does not know this principle and makes no provision for the spleen in advance; he treats only the liver, regarding the spleen as not yet ill and leaving it untreated. To wait until the spleen is ill and then treat it is too late.

What is the method of making the spleen replete? If the disease of the liver calls for supplementation, the organ itself is vacuous and likes its own flavor. Sour enters the liver first, so it supplements. Heart fire is the child of the liver; bitter enters the heart first, and the child can make the mother replete, so scorched bitter assists. The spleen is what the liver overcomes. Using sweet to benefit it seems pointless, but [in fact] spleen earth can restrain kidney water; when kidney water is weak, the heart is unrestrained; heart fire can restrain lung metal, and the lung is damaged by fire. Why does liver wood flourish when the lung is damaged? Because metal is the enemy of wood. When metal is damaged, wood is exuberant. Therefore it says, "the liver recovers by itself." This principle is very subtle, so it says, "This is the essential subtlety of supplementing the spleen to treat the liver."

Yet weakening the kidney, letting the heart run free, and damaging the lung are not in themselves good things. It is only because the liver is vacuous that one straightens what is bent to obtain balance; it is an ingenious method used out of necessity. If the liver has a replete evil, one is about to drain the liver and has no leisure to supplement or assist it, let alone to benefit it in this roundabout way. Therefore it says, "when it is replete, it need not be used." This method is what the classic means by "do not make vacuity more vacuous or repletion more replete; supplement insufficiency and reduce surplus." All the viscera are so, not only the liver. Therefore it says, "the other viscera follow this."

Discussion: When liver wood is vacuous, it should properly be nourished by kidney water. Now it says, "a disease of the liver is supplemented with sour, assisted with bitter, and benefited with sweet": sweet supports earth to restrain water, making fire exuberant so that it damages lung metal, which harms its enemy wood. Then, necessarily, a disease of the lung would be supplemented with acrid, assisted with salty, and benefited with sour, supporting wood to restrain earth and making water exuberant so that it damages heart fire, which harms its enemy metal; a disease of the heart would be supplemented with bitter, assisted with sweet, and benefited with acrid, supporting metal to restrain wood and making earth exuberant so that it damages kidney water, which harms its enemy fire; a disease of the kidney would be supplemented with salty, assisted with sour, and benefited with bitter, supporting fire to restrain metal and making wood exuberant so that it damages spleen earth, which harms its enemy water; a disease of the spleen would be supplemented with sweet, assisted with acrid, and benefited with salty, supporting water to restrain fire and making metal exuberant so that it damages liver wood, which harms its enemy earth. In every case this supports what one overcomes and restrains what one cannot overcome, and on the contrary damages what generates oneself and assists what one generates. How is this the meaning of "when vacuous, supplement its mother"? [So one might object.]

[The answer is that] such an objection does not see that the argument here is concerned only with rescuing the organ that receives the transmission. Therefore it speaks of "treating what is not yet ill." The reason a disease drags on without recovery is not a concern about the vacuity of the organ itself, but a concern that transmission continues without end and the disease deepens, as wood necessarily restrains earth. Therefore "one must first make the spleen replete" is the essential subtlety of treating the liver, and it is the general method of treating all the viscera. Hence supplementing the mother is not as immediate as supplementing the organ itself directly; and further, by assisting its child, the child makes the mother replete and the organ flourishes all the more. Moreover, one supports the spleen earth that liver wood restrains, and in a roundabout way restrains lung metal, the enemy of wood. Since [the liver] is already vacuous and cannot bear further loss, pacifying its enemy is the urgent matter. If one held only to the theory of supplementing the mother and enriched water to generate wood, the child would make the mother replete: kidney water would be assisted and lung metal would become replete, and how much would that harm the liver? "When vacuous, supplement its mother" has a separate explanation. Suppose the liver is ill and vacuous during the four seasonal periods when earth is flourishing; then the theory of making the spleen replete cannot be used. Even outside those periods, if the person's spleen earth is constitutionally strong, may one further benefit the spleen and let it overwhelm the liver? Then one must enrich kidney water to moisten liver wood. Therefore it says, "when vacuous, supplement its mother." The other viscera are likewise.

## Notes

The opening clause of the *Jingui yaolüe*, it sets out the idea of "treating what is not yet ill" (attending first to the organ that the disease has not yet reached), using the relation of liver and spleen as an example.

- By the restraining cycle of the five phases (wood → earth → water → fire → metal → wood), a liver (wood) disease passes to the spleen (earth), so when treating the liver one first supplements the spleen.
- The chain in the second half ("the spleen can damage the kidney ... liver qi is exuberant") traces the restraining cycle to explain how the liver becomes exuberant. Since it makes "weakening the kidney and damaging the lung" part of treatment, it has long been debated; some regard this part as a later commentator's note mixed into the text.
- "In the four seasons the spleen is flourishing" refers to the idea that earth (spleen) governs the last eighteen days of each season.
- "Do not make vacuity more vacuous or repletion more replete" warns against mistreatment (cf. *Nanjing* difficulty 81).
- Xu Bin's edition reads 不必 ("need not") for 不在 in "it is not to be used."
- Xu Bin's "Discussion" raises the objection that applying this reasoning to other organs seems to contradict the principle of "supplementing the mother," and answers it.
