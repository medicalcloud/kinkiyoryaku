# Clause 13 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Question: There are eighteen yang diseases; what does this mean? The master said: Headache, and pulling pain of the nape, lower back, spine, arms, and legs. [Question:] There are eighteen yin diseases; what does this mean? The master said: Cough, ascending qi, dyspnea, hiccup, [pain of the] throat, rumbling intestines, distension and fullness, heart pain, and hypertonicity. The diseases of each of the five zang organs number eighteen, making ninety diseases in all. People also have the six minor [diseases]; each minor has eighteen diseases, making one hundred and eight diseases in all. The five taxations, seven damages, six extremes, and the thirty-six diseases of women are not included among them.

Clear pathogen resides above; turbid pathogen resides below. Great pathogen strikes the exterior; small pathogen strikes the interior. The pathogen of grain foods, entering through the mouth, is abiding food. The five pathogens strike people each according to rules: wind strikes in the morning, cold strikes in the evening; dampness damages below, fog damages above; wind makes the pulse floating, cold makes the pulse tense; fog damages the skin and interstices, dampness flows into the joints; food damages the spleen and stomach; extreme cold damages the meridians, extreme heat damages the collaterals.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

This section first states that disease differs by yin and yang, zang-fu organs, and then that what is contracted differs in how the five pathogens strike people, wishing people to seek [the cause] by cross-reference.

When disease is in the yang, it should be treated from the yang. The head and nape are above, so they are yang. The lower back and spine, though in the middle, are governed by the governing vessel and are also yang. The limbs belong to yang, so the arms and legs are also yang. Yang has three meridians, Taiyang, Shaoyang, and Yangming; combined with the six places, is this not three times six, eighteen? When disease is in the yin, it should be treated from the yin. Cough, ascending qi with dyspnea, hiccup, sore throat, rumbling intestines with distension and fullness, heart pain with hypertonicity — all are diseases within the sanjiao; they are interior and yin. Yin has three meridians, Taiyin, Shaoyin, and Jueyin; combined with the six places, is this not three times six, eighteen? Since yin diseases number eighteen and yin belongs to the zang organs, if each of the five zang organs has eighteen, is this not ninety diseases in all? Since yang diseases number eighteen and yang belongs to the bowels, if each of the six fu organs has eighteen but the diseases are somewhat minor, is this not one hundred and eight diseases in all? The above speaks roughly only of pathogens arriving from outside and striking yin, yang, zang organs, and bowels. We are very far from antiquity and cannot enumerate them disease by disease. For reference, I append the *Lingshu*'s list of the diseases of the five zang organs sought by the six pulses: slowdown, tense, large, small, slippery, and hesitant.

[What follows is from the *Lingshu*, chapter "Pathogenic qi, zang organs, bowels, and disease forms."]

Lung pulse: very tense, epilepsy; slightly tense, lung cold and heat, with lassitude, coughing and spitting blood, pain drawing into the lower back, back, and chest, or nasal polyps blocking [the nose]. Very moderate, profuse sweating; slightly moderate, wilting, fistula, and hemilateral wind, with unstoppable sweating from the neck down. Very large, swelling of the neck; slightly large, lung impediment drawing into the chest and back, with aversion to sunlight. Very small, diarrhea; slightly small, wasting and thirst. Very slippery, rushing respiration with ascending qi; slightly slippery, bleeding above and below. Very rough, vomiting of blood; slightly rough, scrofula between the neck and armpit, the lower part unable to support the upper, and a corresponding liking for sour.

Heart pulse: very tense, tugging and slackening (convulsions); slightly tense, heart pain drawing into the back, and food does not go down. Very moderate, mania with laughter; slightly moderate, hidden beam below the heart, moving up and down, with occasional spitting of blood. Very large, something stuck in the throat; slightly large, heart impediment drawing into the back, with frequent tearing. Very small, frequent hiccup; slightly small, wasting and thirst. Very slippery, frequent thirst; slightly slippery, heart *shan* drawing into the umbilicus, with rumbling in the lesser abdomen. Very rough, loss of voice; slightly rough, overflowing of blood, linking reversal, tinnitus, and epilepsy.

Liver pulse: very tense, raving; slightly tense, fat qi below the ribs like an overturned cup. Very moderate, vomiting; slightly moderate, water conglomeration and impediment. Very large, internal abscess; slightly large, liver impediment, with retraction of the genitals and cough drawing into the lesser abdomen. Very small, drinking much; slightly small, wasting and thirst. Very slippery, prominent *shan*; slightly slippery, enuresis. Very rough, spillage fluid; slightly rough, convulsions and sinew impediment.

Spleen pulse: very tense, convulsions; slightly tense, diaphragm blockage, with food and drink coming back out after entering and spitting of foam. Very moderate, wilting reversal; slightly moderate, wind wilting with the limbs useless, yet the mind is clear as if there were no illness. Very large, falling down as if struck; slightly large, *shan* qi, with large pus and blood in the abdomen outside the intestines and stomach. Very small, cold and heat; slightly small, wasting and thirst. Very slippery, prominent *shan* and dribbling urinary block; slightly slippery, worm toxin and roundworms with heat in the abdomen. [Next] I append the method described in the *Qianjin* of combining needling with the pulse to treat the six fu organs, for reference.

Large intestine disease: cutting pain in the intestines with gurgling; if in winter one is further affected by cold, there is diarrhea with pain at the umbilicus and inability to stand for long. Needle the source of the *huang*, Upper Great Hollow, and Three Li.

Small intestine disease: pain in the lesser abdomen, with the lower back and spine pulling on the testicles in pain, and at times urgency followed [by defecation]; heat in front of the ears, and heat on the shoulders and between the little and ring fingers. Needle Lower Great Hollow, and press the meridians it passes through to regulate them.

Stomach disease: abdominal distension, pain at the stomach duct opposite the heart, propping into both ribsides, blockage of the diaphragm and throat, and food and drink not going down. Needle Three Li.

Gallbladder disease: frequent sighing, bitter taste in the mouth, vomiting of old fluid, a fearful heart as if someone were about to seize him, a feeling of obstruction in the throat, and frequent spitting. Needle Three Li to bring down the counterflow of stomach qi, and needle the blood collaterals of the foot Shaoyang to close off the gallbladder counterflow.

Sanjiao disease: abdominal qi fullness, the lesser abdomen especially hard, inability to urinate with urgency; when it overflows, water is retained and becomes distension. Needle Bend Yang.

Bladder disease: one-sided swelling and pain of the lesser abdomen; pressing it with the hand produces a desire to urinate but nothing comes out; heat on the shoulders and on the outer edge of the little toe, the shin, and behind the ankle. If the pulse is deep, needle Bend Center.

The five taxations, seven damages, and six extremes, and the thirty-six diseases of women, are not diseases in which external pathogen deeply damages the meridians and zang organs, so they are not counted. I now append the five taxations, seven damages, and six extremes described in the *Qianjin* for reference.

The five taxations: prolonged looking damages the blood; prolonged lying damages the qi; prolonged sitting damages the flesh; prolonged standing damages the bones; prolonged walking damages the sinews.

The seven damages: overeating damages the spleen; great anger with counterflow qi damages the liver; forcing oneself to lift heavy things and sitting on damp ground damage the kidney; a cold body and cold drinks damage the lung; worry, sorrow, and preoccupation damage the heart; wind, rain, cold, and summer heat damage the body; great anger and fear without moderation damage the will.

The six extremes: extreme of qi, of blood, of sinew, of bone, of flesh, and of essence.

I further append, for reference, the twelve conglomerations, nine pains, seven harms, five damages, and three causes of women, which make the thirty-six diseases.

The twelve conglomerations refer to what is discharged: first, like green mud; second, like green blood; third, like purple juice; fourth, like red skin; fifth, like scabs of pus; sixth, like bean juice; seventh, like mallow broth; eighth, like congealed blood; ninth, green blood resembling water; tenth, like rice water; eleventh, like menstrual washing water; twelfth, menses not coming at the proper time.

The nine pains: first, pain and injury in the vagina; second, dribbling pain in the vagina; third, pain on urination; fourth, pain from cold; fifth, abdominal pain when the menses come; sixth, pain from qi fullness pouring; seventh, sweating with pain in the genitals as if bitten by insects; eighth, pain below the ribs; ninth, lower back pain.

The seven harms: first, harm to eating; second, harm to qi; third, harm from cold; fourth, harm from taxation; fifth, harm from sexual activity; sixth, harm to pregnancy; seventh, harm to sleep.

The five damages: first, pain of the orifice; second, pain from cold and heat within; third, tense, tight pain of the small intestine; fourth, numbness of the zang organs; fifth, the infant's gate (cervix) not being straight.

The three causes: first, menses blocked and not flowing; second, cessation of childbearing; third, emaciation with no growth of flesh.

But the reasons why pathogen damages only the yang or only the yin, why it is in the exterior or the interior, above or below, or in the spleen and stomach, are merely that pathogens differ in clarity and turbidity and in size, or that some differ only in being food and drink. The time and depth of the damage are also distinguished by when the pathogen strikes. Therefore it says, "The five pathogens strike people each according to rules." The five pathogens are the wind, cold, dampness, fog, and food below.

Wind is a yang pathogen, so it strikes in the morning; morning is the wei-defense [aspect]. Cold is a yin pathogen, so it strikes in the evening; evening is the ying-nutrient [aspect]. Dampness is a turbid pathogen, so it damages below; fog is a clear pathogen, so it damages above. Wind is light and rising by nature, so it makes the pulse floating; cold is contracting and binding by nature, so it makes the pulse tense. Fog is clear and yang by nature, so it runs to the skin and interstices; dampness is yin and turbid by nature, so it flows into the joints. Food and drink are governed by the spleen and stomach, so the damage stays in the spleen and stomach and does not reach the meridians or interstices. Extreme cold damages the meridians: in the winter months yang is not outside, so there is nothing to secure the exterior, and the pathogen's damage reaches the meridians; hence the theory of true cold damage. Extreme heat damages the collaterals: in the summer months yang qi is outside, summer heat joins it, sweat comes out and the collaterals become deficient; hence there are diseases such as prickly heat, boils, and summer heat stroke, but no six-meridian cold damage.

## Notes

A clause on the classification of diseases (eighteen yang diseases, eighteen yin diseases, ninety diseases of the five zang organs, one hundred and eight of the six minor, etc.) and on how the five pathogens (wind, cold, dampness, fog, food) each damage people.

- How the eighteen yang and eighteen yin diseases are counted is not explained in the text, and there have long been various interpretations. Xu Bin counts six yang locations (head, nape, lower back, spine, arms, legs) times three yang meridians, and six yin signs times three yin meridians, but this is only one view.
- From "clear pathogen resides above" on, the clause relates the nature of each pathogen to the place it damages: wind strikes in the morning (yang), cold in the evening (yin); wind makes the pulse floating, cold makes it tense.
- 䅽飥 refers to food made from grain (such as noodles), i.e., disease from food (abiding food). Sources vary (馨飪, 䅽飪).
- The quotations from the *Lingshu* and *Qianjin* in Xu Bin's commentary are broken into fragments by the line breaks of the interlinear notes in the Kanripo text; the translation joins them. The wording follows Xu Bin's text and differs in places from the current editions of the *Lingshu* and *Qianjin*. The translation is tentative.
