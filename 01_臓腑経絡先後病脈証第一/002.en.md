# Clause 2 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Human beings receive the five constants (five phases) and are born and grow by wind-qi. Although wind-qi can generate the myriad things, it can also harm them, as water can float a boat and can also overturn it. If the original true [qi] of the five viscera flows freely, the person is at peace and in harmony. When guest qi and evil wind strike a person, death is frequent.

The thousand kinds of illness and hardship [base: 灾 "calamity"] do not go beyond three categories. First, the channels and network vessels receive evil, which enters the viscera and bowels; this is caused from within. Second, in the four limbs and nine orifices, the blood vessels pass [the evil] on, and there is congestion and blockage; this is being struck at the outer skin. Third, damage by sexual activity, metal blades, insects, and beasts. Examined by this [base: 凡 "broadly"], the origins of disease are exhausted.

If a person can nurture life with caution, not allowing evil wind to offend the channels; if, when [evil] has just struck the channels and not yet flowed on to the bowels and viscera, it is treated at once; if, as soon as heaviness and stagnation are felt in the limbs, one practices guiding and stretching (daoyin), breathing exercises (tuna), acupuncture and moxibustion, and ointment massage, not allowing the nine orifices to become blocked; if, further, one does not violate the king's law and avoids injury by birds and beasts; if one does not allow sexual activity to exhaust and deplete [the essence]; if in food and drink one moderates cold and heat, bitter, sour, acrid, and sweet, leaving the body no weakness — then disease has no way to enter the interstices (couli). The *cou* are where the triple burner connects and gathers the original true [qi], where blood and qi pour. The *li* are the patterned texture of the skin and viscera.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

This section says that although the changing forms of disease are many, their causes are only three, to show that shallow [disease] must not be treated deeply, nor deep [disease] shallowly. Human beings receive the wholeness of yin, yang, and the five phases and differ from other kinds; their birth and growth truly depend on wind and qi. Without the eight winds there would be nothing to stir and harmonize; without the six qi there would be nothing to transform and nurture. Therefore the *Neijing* says, "Wind generates wood, wood generates the liver," and also, "Spirit in heaven is wind." It says, "What heaven [gives] in me is virtue; what earth [gives] in me is qi; virtue flows, qi presses, and life arises," and also, "Yang transforms qi; yin completes form." But where there is right qi there is foul qi; where there is gentle wind there is wild wind. Generating things and harming things come from the same mechanism. Hence the metaphor of floating and overturning a boat.

Speaking of what has form, there are the five viscera; speaking of the formless, there is the original true [qi]. Wind and qi are both things that flow, and the human viscera respond to them; so when they flow freely, there is peace and harmony. The right qi of the four seasons is the host qi; incorrect foul qi is guest qi. Wind that nourishes things is right wind; wind that harms things is evil wind. What has the power to generate things also has the power to harm them, so when it strikes a person death is frequent.

But wind is light or heavy, and disease is shallow or deep. The human body has only an inside and an outside, so in summary, the thousand kinds of affliction do not go beyond three categories. First, evil arises from the channels and viscera, from within and deep; this is caused from within. Second, disease flows from the limbs, orifices, and skin along the blood vessels, and is shallow; this is caused from without. Third, disease arises from the king's law, sexual activity, metal blades, insects, and beasts; though it may gradually reach the channels, it is not a matter of the channels, and though it harms the skin, it is not a matter of the skin; this is the cause neither internal nor external. These are the so-called origins of disease.

If a person is cautious and nurtures life in this respect, not allowing the abnormal qi of wind and cold to offend the channels, there is no disease. If [evil] has just struck the channels and has not yet entered the viscera, it can be cured by sweating, vomiting, or harmonizing; or if it has entered inside but only slightly, it can be cured by purging. This is the so-called "treating it at once," corresponding to the earlier passage on internal causes. If the evil of the six excesses has been felt only slightly in the skin and flows on to the orifices and blood vessels, entering shallowly, then breathing exercises and guiding are like the practices of cultivating perfection, and acupuncture, moxibustion, and ointment massage are like the methods of external medicine; the heaviness and stagnation become free and comfortable, and blockage has no occasion to arise. This corresponds to the earlier passage on external causes. Further, if one does not violate the king's law or meet with injury, there is no unexpected insult. And though there is sexual activity, if it is not allowed to exhaust, the inside is replete and not vacuous. This corresponds to the earlier passage on sexual activity. The several phrases on food and drink say together that one must be cautious in everything concerning food, drink, and daily life.

As for the interstices: whenever disease entangles the body, it does not stop at the channels and blood vessels but inevitably fills the interstices, so one must be cautious so that it has no way in. The *cou* are where the triple burner and the bone joints connect, where blood and qi come and go; hence "where the original true [qi] connects and gathers." The *li*: the skin and viscera, inside and out, all have their texture, fine and orderly; hence "patterned texture."

Discussion: The theory of internal and external causes is Zhongjing's way of making people know the depth at which a disease is contracted, so that they treat accordingly. Thus the later discussion of wind-strike distinguishes evil in the skin, evil in the channels, and evil in the viscera; and the later discussion of obstructed menses says, "Over the years, blood becomes cold and accumulates and binds at the uterine gate; cold damages the channels, congeals and hardens, and above becomes lung abscess." Hence we know that "internal cause" here is distinguished not by internal damage versus external contraction but by the depth of the disease. "In the four limbs and nine orifices the blood vessels pass it on, and there is congestion and blockage" clearly refers to minor diseases such as numbness of the hands, leg qi, pestilential wind, scabies, and all kinds of pain and itching. Seeing that below it says, "as soon as heaviness and stagnation are felt in the limbs," we know that the tone stresses that they are shallow and easy to treat. Sexual activity damages the inside, yet it is listed outside both internal and external causes. This is probably because Zhongjing's discussion takes the striking of wind-qi as its main concern: what enters the viscera from the channels is internal and deep; what flows from the skin into the blood vessels is external and shallow; and damage by sexual activity, unrelated to the channels and skin, is neither internal nor external. That is, the disease is due to vacuity and is not comparable to guest qi and evil wind striking a person. Then we know that treatment should solely supplement the yin and must not offend the channels and blood vessels. The error of later people who use medicinals that move the channels and supplement the blood to treat the vacuity detriment of sexual excess is also evident.

Further discussion: Sun Simiao often said, "Earth, water, fire, and wind combine to form a human being. When fire qi is out of order, the whole body steams with heat; when wind qi is out of order, the whole body becomes stiff and all the pores are blocked; when water qi is out of order, the body swells, the qi is full and the panting coarse; when earth qi is out of order, the limbs cannot be raised and speech has no voice. When fire departs the body is cold; when wind stops the qi is cut off; when water is exhausted there is no blood; when earth disperses the body splits." So wind in a person makes up the physical body together [with the others]. Therefore it says, "Human beings receive the five constants and are born and grow by wind-qi." We know that of the six qi that harm people, wind is the most intimate. But the other five qi only harm and do not benefit, whereas birth and growth depend on wind. Therefore it says both "when evil wind strikes a person, death is frequent" and "wind can generate the myriad things."

## Notes

A general clause that divides the causes of disease into three categories and describes preventing disease by nurturing life and treating early.

- The first is deep disease entering the viscera from the channels (internal cause); the second is shallow disease remaining in the limbs, orifices, and blood vessels (external cause); the third is injury by sexual activity, blades, insects, and beasts. Chen Yan's later theory of the three causes (internal, external, neither internal nor external) is said to derive partly from this clause, though the meanings differ. Xu Bin also explains that "internal" and "external" here are distinguished by depth, not by internal damage versus external contraction.
- *Daoyin* and *tuna* are life-nurturing practices of exercise and breathing; ointment massage (*gaomo*) is rubbing with medicated ointment.
- The definition of *couli* (interstices) here is distinctive to this book and often quoted later.
- In 以此詳之 ("examined by this"), the base reads 凡 for 此, but all other sources read 此, so it has been emended (see the emendation notes). In 千般疢難, the base's 灾 has likewise been emended to 疢 ("illness").
- In 房室勿令竭乏, the base reads 竭之, but most other sources read 竭乏 ("exhaust and deplete"), so it has been emended.
