# Clause 2 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Question: How do the four rheums differ? The master said: When a person who was formerly robust is now thin, and water runs in the intestines with a gurgling sound, it is called phlegm-fluid retention. When after drinking the water flows to below the ribs and coughing and spitting cause pulling pain, it is called pleural fluid retention. When drunk water flows and returns to the four limbs, and there should be sweating but there is none, with aching heaviness of the body, it is called subcutaneous fluid retention. When there is cough with counterflow, leaning to breathe, shortness of breath with inability to lie down, and the body looks swollen, it is called thoracic fluid retention.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

In spleen-stomach patterns there are cases of suddenly gaining and suddenly losing weight, alternating irregularly, unlike this case where once thin one does not recover. Hence "formerly robust, now thin": formerly fat and robust, now suddenly emaciated. Intestinal rumbling may be due to qi deficiency, to fire gnawing, or to cold qi; but in phlegm-fluid retention there is actually retained fluid overflowing downward; hence "water runs in the intestines with a gurgling sound," like a little water in a bag dripping out with a sound.

When after drinking the water flows to below the ribs, there is much water and the qi counterflows, like the water of a waterspout drawn up by the air and unable to descend. The lung governs the spreading of qi; when qi counterflows, the lung qi does not move, so there is coughing and spitting. The qi does not move yet tries to move, attacking each other, hence pulling pain.

Whenever drink enters the stomach, it spreads the essential qi upward to the spleen; the spleen qi disperses essence upward to the lung, which regulates the waterways and conveys downward to the bladder; the water essence spreads in four directions, and the five meridians move together. If much water is drunk, water being cold by nature, the qi counterflows, and counterflow causes overflow, so it flows to the four limbs. But with sweating it would disperse. Without sweating, the body takes on dampness, the wei-defensive qi does not move, and it is heavy; and it also receives pathogenic cold, which contends with the right, causing aching. This is due to sudden overflow of water qi, hence "subcutaneous fluid retention." The *Neijing* says, "When the liver pulse is soft and scattered and the complexion lustrous, there should be subcutaneous fluid retention"; water overflowing makes wood float and become lustrous; it details this by complexion and pulse together.

If retained fluid pathogen pours one-sidedly and lodges in the winding places of the upper jiao — the branch vessel of the lung connects with the large intestine, and the large intestine meridian rises and falls from the meeting at the cervical vertebrae into the supraclavicular fossa, connects with the lung, and descends through the diaphragm — then where retained fluid lodges, it neither connects outward to the exterior nor follows inward the pathway of food and drink, but obstructs the qi pathway linking lung and large intestine. The lung governs qi, and qi likes to descend smoothly; obstructed, it counterflows, and counterflow causes cough. Breath is named from respiration; when qi counterflows and there is cough, one leans to breathe. Leaning is as if stopping to lean, slightly hurried; with such stopping and leaning, the ancestral qi does not spread, so breath is short. Yangming qi descends when smooth and ascends when counterflowing; counterflowing upward, one cannot lie down — what is called "Yangming counterflows and cannot follow its path." Looking swollen is not swelling; the qi counterflows and floats temporarily, and when the dyspnea settles it is flat again.

Discussion: Pleural fluid retention and subcutaneous fluid retention are sudden diseases. Pleural fluid retention governs the inside, so it hurts and can be purged; subcutaneous fluid retention governs the outside, so it is heavy and can be sweated. Phlegm-fluid retention varies in severity and duration, so it need not center on pain or heaviness. Thoracic fluid retention generally does not mention pain, and the pulse becomes wiry. Chest bi-impediment is also said to have "dyspnea, coughing and spitting, shortness of breath, or inability to lie down," but mostly with chest and back pain and a deep pulse. So the distinction between chest bi-impediment and thoracic fluid retention lies entirely in pain and the wiry pulse. Thoracic fluid retention is one-sided and slight in momentum, so the pulse is wiry without pain, and each is treated according to the signs. Chest bi-impediment is deficient and large in momentum, with the pathogen bound, so the pulse is deep with pain, and treatment centers only on opening binding and moving yang. Some thoracic fluid retention also has a deep, wiry pulse, but the emphasis is on accompanying signs, and it is not true thoracic fluid retention; see the clauses below.

## Notes

A clause on the signs of each of the four rheums.

- Phlegm-fluid retention: a formerly stout person becomes thin, with water sounds in the intestines (water in the stomach and intestines).
- Pleural fluid retention: water collects below the ribs, with pulling pain on coughing (often identified with pleural effusion).
- Subcutaneous fluid retention: water overflows into the limbs, with no sweating and aching heaviness (edema).
- Thoracic fluid retention: cough with leaning to breathe, inability to lie down, and an appearance of swelling (water of heart and lung; some identify it with heart failure and the like).
