# Clause 7 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

When water is in the kidney, there are palpitations below the heart.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

The signs of the four rheums have already been clearly distinguished above. But the five zang organs of a person may be one-sidedly deficient, and where deficient, disease pathogen takes advantage. So all are said to "be in" [the organ]. One should naturally distinguish and treat according to the signs, not stick rigidly to rules.

The heart governs fire; water presses on it, so the qi contracts and pounds as if attacking. Hardness is the image of congealed yin. Shortness of breath is because the heart qi is suppressed and the ancestral qi weak, so exhalation is naturally short. Disliking water and not wanting to drink: water is originally the enemy of fire, and when water is abundant, the dislike increases.

The lung is clear and descending by nature, moving the ying-nutrient and wei-defense and spreading the fluids. When pathogenic water blocks it, the qi is depressed and drool gathers, like the foamy drool spat in atrophy of the lung lobes. But the qi is depressed and hot, and the fluids are lost again, so one still draws on water to save oneself.

The spleen governs the flesh and dislikes dampness. Receiving water qi, it becomes soggy, stagnant, and heavy. When the spleen essence does not move, the central qi is insufficient, with lassitude and shallow breathing.

The liver is exterior-interior with the Shaoyang gallbladder, and governs the half exterior half interior because their meridians run together along the ribsides. When water qi takes advantage, yin cold binds within, so below the ribs there is propping fullness. The Shaoyang qi goes up and out, so it strikes and causes sneezing, as if damaged by wind. But attacking each other and pulling, there is pain. Propping fullness means the chest is not entirely full but full on one side.

The kidney is originally the water organ; adding water makes it doubly strong, so it encroaches on the heart, causing unrest and palpitations. Palpitations may also be due to heart deficiency, but where thoracic fluid retention shows this sign as well, the water should be drained.

Discussion: Since the location of water is not fixed, why speak of the zang organs and not the bowels? The bowels belong to yang, and in the bowels [water] moves. The zang organs belong to yin, and water is of a kind with yin, so it stagnates long. Phlegm-fluid retention is in the chest and seems not to belong to the zang organs; but where there is deficiency, pathogen is received, and each disease has its point of attachment, so they draw on each other and do not leave. Note: dividing water among the five zang organs here differs from the five clauses on heart water, lung water, etc. in the chapter on water qi, and they should be compared. There, water is discussed as water of the whole body: the genuine qi of the zang organ is first ill, blocking the waterways so that water moves wildly; so water swelling is the main disease, and it is called directly "heart water" and so on, meaning it arises from the heart. But water qi is received by both upper and lower jiao, and the signs differ according to where the water comes from. So spleen and kidney, in the lower jiao, both have abdominal fullness and inhibited urination, while only the liver at times passes urine. Heart and lung are in the upper jiao; the visceral qi acts as agent and gradually reaches the middle and lower; so from the heart there are heaviness, shallow breathing, and genital swelling, and from the lung there are body swelling, duck-stool diarrhea, and difficult urination — all images of soaking into spleen and kidney. Here, what is spoken of is retained fluid of water that has entered inward. When one of the five zang organs happens to be one-sidedly deficient and the retained fluid qi attacks it, retained fluid is the main disease, and it is called "water is in," meaning the retained fluid qi reaches it. Though the retained fluid is in the upper jiao, where the water goes differs, so the signs differ. In heart and lung it should of course appear in the upper jiao. In the liver: the liver is below, but its residence is the ribsides; the disease is through the residence and the qi flows to the zang organ, so the ribsides are full and sneezing causes pain. The spleen is below, but it governs the central qi and the flesh; when retained fluid qi is in surplus and disease qi affects the spleen, water is in the spleen, with heaviness and shallow breathing. The kidney is below, but heart and kidney originally communicate; when the heart has first become deficient and phlegm-fluid retention lodges there, and the disease qi affects the kidney, water is in the kidney and encroaches on the heart, causing palpitations.

## Notes

The signs of water in the kidney.

- Xu Bin's commentary explains the five-zang organs water clauses of units 3 through 7 together and discusses how they differ from the clauses on heart water, lung water, etc. in the later chapter on water qi.
