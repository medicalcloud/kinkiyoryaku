# Clause 25 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Wind and dampness contending, with the bones and joints aching and vexed, pulling pain so that one cannot bend or stretch, pain that becomes severe when [anything] comes near, sweating, shortness of breath, inhibited urination, aversion to wind with unwillingness to take off clothes, or slight swelling of the body: Gancao Fuzi tang (Kanzobushito; Licorice and Aconite Decoction) governs it.

Gancao Fuzi tang formula

Gancao (kanzo) 2 liang (mix-fried); fuzi (bushi) 2 pieces (blast-fried, remove the skin); baizhu (byakujutsu) 2 liang; guizhi (keishi) 4 liang (remove the bark).

Of the four ingredients above, with 6 sheng of water boil down to 3 sheng, remove the dregs, and take 1 sheng warm, three times a day. If after the first dose a slight sweat is obtained, it resolves. For one who can eat but sweats and becomes vexed again, take 5 ge. For one for whom 1 sheng may be too much, 6 or 7 ge is best.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

This speaks of wind-damp with severe impediment and much pain. Wind and dampness contend, so that the bones and joints ache and are vexed, and the pulling pain is severe; this is wind entering and increasing the force. Being unable to bend or stretch, with the pain becoming severe when [anything] comes near, means bones and flesh all ache, the pain has reached its extreme, and there is impediment. Hence the external dampness causes sweating, the internal dampness causes shortness of breath, and the qi does not diffuse and transform, so urination is inhibited. Moreover, the interior is deficient, so there is aversion to wind and unwillingness to take off clothes. The body is puffed up by the wind qi and slightly swollen; cold-damp predominates and yang does not move. So zhu, fuzi, and gancao strengthen the qi of the intestines and stomach, and guizhi greatly moves the yang.

Comparing this with the previous Qu Gui jia Baizhu tang: there, with no retching, no thirst, and normal stool and urine, guizhi was removed, and only shengjiang (shokyo) and dazao (taiso) were used to diffuse the qi of the upper jiao, relying on the great power of fuzi to move the dampness. Here, inside and outside, bone and flesh, there is impediment everywhere, which shengjiang and dazao cannot diffuse and free; so shengjiang and dazao are not used and guizhi is added. This means that only this can move the qi of the ying-nutrient and wei-defense and open the fixed impediment.

Discussion: Dampness may change in the course of disease, may soak in gradually, may turn into heat, may be heat below with cold still in the chest, or dampness above with cold still below. In general, dampness is sticky and stagnant by nature; carrying wind it goes upward; due to deficiency or cold it obstructs one-sidedly; and when it accumulates long it becomes fixed impediment. So Zhongjing first set out Taiyang disease turning into damp impediment (after illness), next body aches turning yellow (long illness), and also cold above with heat below (due to deficiency obstructing one-sidedly, so that between upper and lower it is uncertain which is heat and which cold). The crucial point of life lies in the original qi within, so throughout he warns against purging and forbids draining, and the method of treatment is mainly sweating and percolating dampness. If there is fixed impediment outside, supplementing is added; if there is accumulated cold inside, warming is added. The formulas given are six in all, reducible to three methods. Mahuang jia Zhu tang (Maokajutsuto) and Ma Xing Yiyi Gancao tang (Maokyoninyokuikanzoto) are the sweating method. Fangji Huangqi tang (Boiogito) is the method of opening impediment and percolating dampness. Guizhi Fuzi tang (Keishibushito) and Qu Gui jia Baizhu Fuzi tang are the method of moving dampness and warming below. Disinhibiting urination and putting [medicine] into the nose are not given formulas, but these have fixed methods.

Dongyuan, for swelling of the scrotum, devised Shengyang Chushi tang, using shengma, chaihu (saiko), qianghuo, duhuo (dokukatsu), gaoben, fangfeng (bofu), gancao, and manjingzi to raise and disperse the dampness, and danggui, huangqi (ogi), and cangzhu to cultivate the governing qi. For dampness with headache he devised Qianghuo Shengshi tang, using qianghuo, duhuo, jingjie, fangfeng, shengma, and chaihu, together with huangqin (ogon), zhuling (chorei), and the like to clear heat and transform dampness. These can supplement where Zhongjing falls short. The *Neijing* says, "When due to dampness, the head feels as if wrapped. When damp-heat is not removed, the large sinews shrink and shorten and the small sinews slacken and lengthen; shrinking and shortening is hypertonicity, slackening and lengthening is wilting. When due to qi, there is swelling." Zhongjing does not mention this, but changes of dampness are discussed and treated as wilting or swelling. "When dampness prevails, there is soggy diarrhea" and "when dampness prevails, there is no desire to eat" are also not mentioned; these all occur among damp conditions but are not definite signs for verifying dampness. I once treated a person with long-standing dampness carrying wind-phlegm, whose body ached and was numb and who could not eat, with fuling (bukuryo), banxia (hange), zisu, houpo (koboku), xiebai, gualou, and the like; he recovered after two prescriptions. Dampness must not be purged, but phlegm stagnation should be cleared.

## Notes

A clause using Gancao Fuzi tang when wind-damp has reached the bones and joints with severe pain. The same clause appears in the lower Taiyang chapter of the *Shanghan lun* (corresponding to clause 175).

- The joints have pulling pain and cannot bend or stretch, the pain increases when one tries to touch them, and there are sweating, shortness of breath, inhibited urination, aversion to wind, and slight swelling.
- Gancao Fuzi tang consists of four ingredients, gancao, fuzi, baizhu, and guizhi, and is said to warm the yang of exterior and interior and remove dampness.
- Xu Bin's "Discussion" summarizes the clauses and formulas on dampness, dividing the six formulas into three methods: sweating, opening impediment and percolating dampness, and moving dampness and warming below. In the latter part he discusses Li Dongyuan's formulas and his own case.
