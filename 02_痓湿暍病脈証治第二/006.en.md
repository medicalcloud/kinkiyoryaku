# Clause 6 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Those with chronic sores, even if the body aches, must not be made to sweat; if sweat comes out, there will be tetany.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

Tetany is generally due to being struck by wind, cold, and dampness, but tracing its cause, it is mostly due to blood loss: the sinews have nothing to nourish them, and the pathogen can attack them. So Zhongjing again traces the origin of tetany. In Taiyang disease, if cold truly predominates, sweating is originally appropriate, but if [the sweating] is excessive, the blood is damaged and cannot nourish the sinews, and there is tetany. If the disease belongs to wind, it should not be purged; purging it doubly damages the yin and causes tetany; and if sweating is also induced, both yin and yang are damaged and there is hypertonicity. In those with chronic sores the blood is already deficient and dry; if the aching is taken for wind and sweating is induced, the fluids are lost and the sinews become dry and cannot be harmonized, and this too becomes tetany. Although pathogen may take advantage after sweating or purging, the main factor is yin deficiency and fluid desertion; so the causes of tetany are detailed in this way.

Discussion: Tetany often occurs after childbirth because of yin deficiency and fluid desertion. Mistaken sweating or purging after childbirth may also cause it, so Zhongjing gives no separate formula but leaves it to the practitioner's judgment. If there is also retching and inability to eat, harmonizing with Xiao Chaihu tang (Minor Bupleurum Decoction) is the mainstay. Guo Jizhong's separate theory of treating postpartum tetany with Xiao Xuming tang (Minor Life-Prolonging Decoction) refers to cases with much pathogen and severe illness; it is not generally appropriate.

Wind attack patterns often with opisthotonos also resemble tetany. But in the rigidity of wind attack there are never Taiyang signs beforehand, and the pulse is always floating and large, not deep, thready, wiry, and slow. Therefore the *Neijing* says, "All sudden rigidity belongs to wind." But since yang governs movement and yin governs stillness, rigidity with stillness should be considered mainly dampness, and rigidity with convulsions should be considered wind. This is the method of distinguishing in treating wind attack. The *Qianjin*'s statements that in warm disease heat entering the kidney also causes tetany, and that in children's epilepsy exuberant heat also causes tetany, also belong to the category of wind attack.

The *Nanzhi* (Wang Haogu's *Cishi nanzhi*) says, "The five kinds of tetany in cold damage all belong to Taiyang. If the head is lowered and looking down, the hands and feet pull, and the elbows and knees lock together, it is Yangming tetany. If one eye, left or right, together with one hand and one foot, convulses, it is Shaoyang tetany." Greater yang certainly belongs to wind-cold, but Yangming and Shaoyang are also wind, fire, and heat arising within, in the category of wind attack. All should be treated by also nourishing yin and clearing heat. As for the tetany discussed here, although the externally contracted wind, cold, and dampness differ, it is the same in being due to yin collapse and dryness of the sinews.

## Notes

A clause stating that people who have long suffered from sores must not be made to sweat even if the body aches. The same prohibition appears in the *Shanghan lun* (middle Taiyang chapter, corresponding to clause 85).

- Those with chronic sores have already lost blood and fluids through pus and blood, so sweating further loses fluids, leaving the sinews unnourished and causing tetany.
- Xu Bin's commentary treats the preceding three clauses (excessive sweating, purging, sweating sore sufferers) together, explaining that the cause of tetany lies in yin deficiency and fluid desertion. In the latter part he also discusses postpartum tetany, opisthotonos in wind attack, and Wang Haogu's Yangming and Shaoyang tetany.
- Guo Jizhong was a Song-dynasty obstetric physician known for his discussions in the *Chanyu baoqing ji*.
