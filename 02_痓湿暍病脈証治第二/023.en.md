# Clause 23 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Wind-damp with a floating pulse, heavy body, sweating, and aversion to wind: Fangji Huangqi tang (Boiogito; Stephania and Astragalus Decoction) governs it.

Fangji Huangqi tang formula

Fangji (boi; stephania root) 1 liang; gancao (kanzo) half a liang (stir-fried); baizhu (byakujutsu) 7.5 *qian*; huangqi (ogi; astragalus) 1 liang 1 *fen* (remove the rhizome head).

Cut the above into pieces the size of hemp seeds or beans; for each dose scoop 5 *qianbi*, add 4 slices of shengjiang (shokyo) and 1 dazao (taiso), decoct with one and a half cups of water down to eight-tenths, remove the dregs, and take warm. After a good while take it again. For dyspnea, add mahuang (mao) half a liang; for disharmony in the stomach, add shaoyao (shakuyaku) 3 *fen*; for surging qi, add guizhi (keishi) 3 *fen*; for old cold below, add xixin (saishin; asarum) 3 *fen*. After taking it, there should be a feeling like insects crawling in the skin, and from the lower back down it is like ice. Afterward, sit on bedding, and for a long time [read: further] wrap a quilt around the body from the lower back down to keep it warm and induce a slight sweat; then recovery.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

This says that in wind-damp there are cases in which the spleen qi cannot move the dampness and [the dampness] does not diminish with sweating; then one must not cling to the rule of inducing a slight sweat. In the previous clause, "aching all over the body" means that although the pathogen is throughout the body, the healthy qi can still function, and the fever shows the tendency is still outward. If the body is heavy, the dampness governs the qi of the flesh. With a floating pulse, sweating, and aversion to wind, the pathogen seems still to be in the exterior; but if the dampness is not resolved by sweating and the body remains heavy, the dampness tries to bind the wind, but the wind-heat is exuberant and is not bound, and instead [the dampness] binds the healthy qi of the flesh. Clearly the spleen and stomach have long been deficient, the right cannot overcome the pathogen, and the external wind and internal dampness are at a standoff.

So zhu and gancao, which fortify the spleen and strengthen the stomach, are the mainstay; huangqi is added to strengthen the wei-defensive qi; and the single ingredient fangji drives out the wind-damp throughout the body. The pathogenic damp of body aches and fever is still in the sinews and interstices, whereas here the healthy qi is impeded by dampness. So that [previous formula] used yiyi and mix-fried gancao to calm the inside, assisting where mahuang and xingren (kyonin) fall short; this one instead uses huangqi, zhu, and gancao as the mainstay, cooperating with fangji to search out the external wind-damp. When dampness has made the body heavy, even with a floating pulse, sweating, and aversion to wind, one must not disperse it through the exterior. Yet the shengjiang is plentiful and the dazao few; the idea of diffusing and dispersing is contained in this.

Fangji Huangqi tang formula: fangji 1 liang; huangqi 1 liang; gancao 5 *qian* (stir-fried, mix-fried); baizhu 7 *qian* 5 *fen*.

... -tenths, remove the dregs, and take warm. For dyspnea, add mahuang 5 *qian*. For disharmony in the stomach, add ...

... Afterward, sit on bedding, and further wrap a quilt around the body from the lower back down to keep it warm and induce a slight sweat; then recovery.

## Notes

A clause using Fangji Huangqi tang for wind-damp with exterior deficiency (sweating and aversion to wind) and heaviness of the body.

- In Fangji Huangqi tang, fangji is said to remove wind-damp, huangqi to secure the exterior, and baizhu and gancao to supplement the spleen and move dampness.
- The base writes 防巳; the correct form is 防已, used in the Japanese translation. Xu Bin's edition writes huangqi as 黄茋.
- The amounts in *qian* and wording such as "cut into pieces the size of hemp seeds or beans" and "one and a half cups of water" are the Song boiled-powder format, as in the previous Ma Xing Yi Gan tang (Makyoyokukanto).
- For 久 in 久以一被繞腰以下, all other sources read 又 ("further"), and 久 appears to be an error.
- The sensations after taking it ("like insects crawling in the skin," "like ice from the lower back down") are said to be reactions of the dampness moving as the medicine takes effect.
- The three fragments at the end of Xu Bin's commentary are parts of the formula and administration in Xu Bin's edition that have entered the commentary, with portions missing.
