# Clause 22 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

When a sick person aches all over the body with fever that becomes severe in the late afternoon, it is called wind-damp. This disease is caused by being exposed to wind while sweating, or by long-term damage from taking cold. Mahuang Xingren Yiyi Gancao tang (Ephedra, Apricot Kernel, Coix, and Licorice Decoction) may be given.

Mahuang Xingren Yiyi Gancao tang formula

Mahuang (remove the nodes, half a liang, soaked in hot water); gancao 1 liang (mix-fried); yiyiren (coix seed) half a liang; xingren 10 pieces (remove the skins, stir-fried over fire).

Cut the above into pieces the size of hemp seeds or beans. For each dose take 4 *qian* 7 [read: 4 *qianbi*]; with one and a half cups of water boil down to eight-tenths, remove the dregs, and take warm. When there is a slight sweat, avoid wind.

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

This says that when dampness leans toward wind and gradually becomes fixed within, one should induce a slight sweat to stop its inward entry and pacify the liver and spleen. When dampness flows into the joints, the pain stays in the joints. Aching all over with fever means the dampness has steamed and become depressed throughout the body from the skin and hair, not stopping at the joints. But it has not yet spread into the flesh, so the body is not heavy. The wind is bound by the dampness, so there is no sweating. It becomes especially severe in the late afternoon; the late afternoon is the *shen* and *you* hours, the qi of metal, governed by the lung. The lung's counterpart is the skin and hair; clearly the wind-damp soaks in through the lung's counterpart and becomes fixed within, and at the time when lung metal flourishes it aids the pathogen in its harm and worsens; this differs from dampness received from below. Hence "this is wind-damp."

But when the skin and hair receive pathogen, how does wind come to carry dampness? Hence we know it is caused by being exposed to wind while sweating, or by long-term damage from taking cold. So mahuang and xingren disinhibit the lung qi and induce a slight sweat to clear the pathogen in the skin and hair. But lung disease inevitably passes to the liver, and the skin and hair inevitably extend to the flesh, so yiyi and mix-fried gancao strengthen the sinews and please the spleen, removing wind and overcoming dampness. Compared with the previous formula, it removes guizhi and zhu and adds yiyi; only mix-fried gancao is large, and the other ingredients are generally light. Since the treatment is in the upper part, its dosage is made small.

[Xu Bin's formula:] mahuang 5 *qian*; xingren 10 pieces (remove the skins and tips); yiyi 5 *qian*; gancao 1 liang (mix-fried).

## Notes

A clause using Mahuang Xingren Yiyi Gancao tang (Ma Xing Yi Gan tang) for "wind-damp" with whole-body aches and fever worsening in the late afternoon.

- The causes given are exposure to wind while sweating and prolonged exposure to cold (interpreted as cold food and drink, cold-water bathing, etc.).
- The formula is like Mahuang tang with guizhi replaced by yiyiren, in small amounts.
- The dose unit *qian* and wording such as "cut into pieces the size of hemp seeds or beans" and "one and a half cups of water" belong to the Song-dynasty boiled-powder (*zhusan*) format and appear to be later alterations.
- For 七 in 四銭七, most other sources read 匕, and it appears to be an error for 四銭匕 ("four *qianbi*," a spoon measure).
