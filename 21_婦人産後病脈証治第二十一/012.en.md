# Clause 12 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Neibu Danggui Jianzhong tang from the *Qianjin*: treats a woman after childbirth who is deficient, emaciated, and insufficient, with unceasing stabbing pain in the abdomen, gasping shallow breathing, or suffering from tension in the lesser abdomen with a rubbing pain radiating to the lower back [base: 皆], and inability to eat or drink. In the month after childbirth, it is good to take four or five doses a day. It makes one strong; it is suitable.

Danggui 4 liang; guizhi (keishi) 3 liang; shaoyao (shakuyaku) 6 liang; shengjiang (shokyo) 3 liang; gancao (kanzo) 2 liang; dazao (taiso) 12 pieces.

Of the six ingredients above, with 1 dou of water boil down to 3 sheng and take warm in three doses, finishing it within a day. If there is great deficiency, add 6 liang of malt sugar; when the decoction is done, add it and warm over the fire to dissolve the sugar. If blood loss is excessive, with flooding [injury] and internal bleeding that do not stop, add 6 liang of dihuang and 2 liang of ejiao (akyo), making eight ingredients; add the ejiao when the decoction is done. If there is no danggui, substitute xiongqiong; if there is no shengjiang, substitute ganjiang (kankyo).

## Translation of Xu Bin's commentary

Guizhi tang (Keishito) is a sage formula for harmonizing ying-nutrient and wei-defense and regulating yin and yang in wind attack patients. Adding malt sugar to make Jianzhong tang skillfully establishes, for those with exuberant pathogen and deficient healthy qi, a method of treating the root first and the branch later. Now, deficiency, emaciation, and insufficiency after childbirth begin with yin deficiency and later include yang deficiency: supplementing yin congeals cold, and supplementing yang congests qi. The acquired constitution takes the center qi as primary, so the method of treatment also comes from Jianzhong. But adding danggui leans it toward the interior; hence "Neibu (internally supplementing) Danggui Jianzhong tang." Unceasing stabbing pain in the abdomen is scant blood; gasping shallow breathing is weak yang. So the acrid warmth of guizhi, shengjiang, and danggui moves the qi of ying-nutrient and wei-defense, gancao and white shaoyao nourish the blood of spleen yin, and malt sugar and dazao drastically supplement the center qi; then the original qi recovers by itself, the emaciated fill out, and the pain stops. Guizhi reaches everywhere in yin and yang, inside and outside, and danggui in particular is good at entering yin and treating disorders below the girdle; so it also governs tension in the lesser abdomen with rubbing pain radiating to the lower back and inability to eat or drink — for when disorders below the girdle are removed, the center qi strengthens by itself. "In the month after childbirth, it is good to take four or five doses a day" means one should promptly regulate with this, so as to avoid regretting a missed opportunity. Yet its ingredients are mild, able both to treat illness and to regulate and supplement; so it also says, "It makes one strong; it is suitable." It says "for great deficiency add malt sugar" and does not use renshen (ninjin) because renshen supplements the original qi but may not sit well with the center qi, whereas malt sugar supplements the center qi and lets the original qi arise by itself. So it is called "Jianzhong" (building the center) because of this one ingredient, precisely because after childbirth the blood is deficient first, and renshen, leaning toward qi, would inevitably make yang suddenly prevail and further damage yin. If blood loss is excessive, with flooding injury and internal bleeding, gandihuang (kanjio) and ejiao are then added because the damage leans toward yin, so yin medicinals are especially added in quantity; it does not mean that dihuang and ejiao must be used after every childbirth.

Discussion: Recently, Shenqi wan (Jinkigan) and Shiquan Dabu tang (Juzentaihoto) both use rougui (cinnamon bark), which does no harm because the warming ingredient is mixed among yin-enriching medicinals. As for Guizhi tang, because it was made the first formula for cold damage, and because there is a doctrine forbidding guizhi in spring and summer, later people do not use it except for the single pattern of sweating, fever, and aversion to cold, and some even keep the prohibition in spring and summer and dare not use it. They do not know that the ancients used guizhi for its power to diffuse and free qi and blood, as a guide for the other medicinals; even Shenqi wan anciently used guizhi, intending not merely to warm the lower part. Further, in the *Jingui*, seven of the ten formulas for deficiency detriment use guizhi; before birth Guizhi tang is used to secure the fetus and Gui Ling tang to remove masses; for postpartum wind attack with a red face, guizhi and fuzi (bushi) are used together; for vexation, agitation, and retching counterflow in a nursing mother, Zhupi Da wan is used with guizhi added to treat heat vexation; and here danggui is added to Jianzhong to make an internal supplement. Then is guizhi not a medicine of general use? Rougui, on the other hand, is hot by nature and reaches downward, and should not be used except for deficiency cold of the lower jiao; yet people instead treat it as of general use, so no wonder they often err in using it. Since I devoted myself to the *Jingui*, its use of guizhi to achieve effects has been endlessly varied and extraordinary, beyond compare. I have picked out one point here to dispel the confusion of people of our time.

## Notes

An appended formula of this chapter: the *Qianjin* Neibu Danggui Jianzhong tang for postpartum deficiency and emaciation with stabbing abdominal pain and the like.

- It is Xiao Jianzhong tang (Shokenchuto) (Guizhi tang with doubled shaoyao plus malt sugar), with the malt sugar optional and danggui added.
- For 皆 in the base's 腰皆, three other sources read 背 ("back"), which the translation follows. After 崩 in the base there is a blank space, and all other sources read 崩傷 ("flooding injury").
- The latter part of Xu Bin's commentary is a discussion of the use of guizhi.
